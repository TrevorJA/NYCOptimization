# Study Motivation: Verified Literature Summaries

Precise one- to two-sentence summaries of the citations that motivate the NYC
re-optimization study, each with the way it supports or constrains the study. Every
summary was verified against the paper's full text in the Zotero library on 2026-09-02
(page-located quotes in the session's verification report). Rows marked UNVERIFIED rest on
metadata, abstracts, or secondary sources only. Use these summaries, not paraphrases from
memory, wherever the proposal or manuscript characterizes a prior study. Terminology
follows `docs/terminology.md`.

## Motivation in one paragraph

Many-objective re-optimization of reservoir operations evaluates each candidate policy by
simulation over an ensemble of streamflow realizations, and the policies that emerge from
the search inherit the hydrologic conditions that ensemble contains. Prior work shows that
the scenarios used during the search phase govern the robustness of the resulting
solutions after re-evaluation (Watson and Kasprzyk, 2017; Trindade et al., 2017; Eker and
Kwakkel, 2018; Bartholomew and Kwakkel, 2020), that robustness frameworks name the
sampling of states of the world as a determinant of any robustness result (Herman et al.,
2015; McPhail et al., 2018, 2020), and that policies evaluated on the historical record or
on a single synthetic ensemble overfit its sequence of extremes and fail out of sample
(Brodeur et al., 2020; Giuliani et al., 2021; Taylor et al., 2024; Leitman et al., 2024).
Existing methods for constructing a search ensemble either stratify on annual flow
(Zatarain Salazar et al., 2017), select scenarios by the regret of the baseline policy
(Cohen et al., 2021), or prescribe drought attributes through the generator (Herman et
al., 2016; Zaniolo et al., 2023, 2024). None constructs a search ensemble to cover a
multi-dimensional space of drought and flood hazards characterized from the streamflow
sequences alone and then tests, on a common re-evaluation ensemble, whether that
construction yields more robust Pareto-approximate policies than Monte Carlo sampling from
the same generator. The Delaware River Basin under the Flexible Flow Management Program is
a test case whose current operating rules were designed by simulation over the historical
record under a single drought-risk constraint (Kolesar and Serio, 2011), after a 1960s
drought emergency that forced the Decree Parties to curtail diversions and reduce flow
targets (Hogarty, 1970; Kolesar and Serio, 2011; Amestoy et al., 2026).

## Scenario choice during search shapes the resulting policies

| Citation | Precise summary | Support for this study |
|---|---|---|
| Eker and Kwakkel (2018) | Ran separate many-objective searches under a reference scenario and under four deep-uncertainty scenarios chosen for policy relevance and outcome diversity on the stylized lake model, then scored every candidate over 1,000 sampled scenarios with signal-to-noise and maximum-regret metrics. Multi-scenario search yielded more robust solutions and a wider variety of robustness trade-offs than the reference scenario alone, and the robustness of a candidate depended on the scenario under which it was found, but four arbitrarily chosen scenarios produced results the authors judged not significantly different from the systematically selected set, an outcome they attributed to features of the lake model. | Establishes that what the search sees determines what it finds, and cautions that curated selection is not automatically better than random, so the HF design must demonstrate its advantage on re-evaluated robustness. Their scenarios were single deep-uncertainty points used in separate searches, not jointly evaluated ensembles. Never summarize this study as showing that scenario selection "does not matter". |
| Bartholomew and Kwakkel (2020) | Compared MORDM search under a reference scenario, multi-scenario MORDM with four additional reference scenarios, and many-objective robust optimization on a fixed 50-scenario Latin hypercube ensemble across three policy formulations of the lake problem, re-evaluating all solutions on 10,000 sampled scenarios. Robustness after re-evaluation rose with the degree to which robustness entered the search, the hypervolume attainable in any single reference scenario fell, and the robust-optimization variant cost six to ten times the function evaluations of multi-scenario search, so the authors recommended multi-scenario MORDM as the pragmatic choice. | Identifies ensemble-based search as the formulation that most reliably preserves robustness after re-evaluation, shows that its cost is set by ensemble size, and names the open question this study answers, which scenarios a small ensemble should contain. The price of robustness in policy search is cited to this paper. |
| Watson and Kasprzyk (2017) | Extended MORDM for the Lower Rio Grande Valley water market by re-running the many-objective search separately under five single-point scenarios, a baseline and four that scenario discovery had identified as vulnerable, and cross-evaluating every solution set under the other scenarios. Solutions optimized under the severe scenarios remained feasible across a wider range of conditions than solutions optimized under the baseline, whose previously identified robust solution failed every re-evaluation. | Supports placing stressing conditions in the search. They optimized under single severe scenarios in separate runs, whereas this study places hazards inside one jointly evaluated ensemble. |
| Trindade et al. (2017) | Contrasted MORDM search that evaluated Research Triangle drought portfolios over 1,000 synthetic streamflow realizations with 13 deeply uncertain factors held at base values against search that paired each realization with one of 1,000 sampled factor vectors inside every evaluation, then re-evaluated both solution sets on 10,000 sampled states of the world with a utility-elicited satisficing criterion. Sampling deep uncertainties during search produced far more candidates, raised the attainable robustness of three of four utilities at the expense of average cost, and exposed and helped resolve the robustness conflicts among the utilities. | Direct evidence that the stress placed on policies during search governs re-evaluated robustness, the mechanism this study tests by varying hydrologic hazard coverage instead of deep-uncertainty factors. |
| Zaniolo et al. (2023) | Optimized dynamic infrastructure and drought-response policies for Santa Barbara on synthetic 100-year streamflow ensembles whose drought persistence, intensity, and frequency were prescribed, and found that the prescribed drought characteristics directly set installed capacity, technology mix, and the state variables and thresholds that trigger actions, with intensity dominating cost and reliability outcomes. | Shows that drought attributes of the search ensemble propagate into both infrastructure and rule triggers, supporting the expectation that hazard coverage will shape FFMP release and diversion rules. |
| Bertsimas and Sim (2004), UNVERIFIED beyond the abstract | Formalized the price of robustness as the nominal objective value forgone to guarantee feasibility under bounded data perturbation and introduced a tunable budget of uncertainty that trades that price against the probability of constraint violation. | Supplies the term for the loss of nominal performance accepted for protection against adverse conditions. Cite for the concept only, and cite Bartholomew and Kwakkel (2020) for the empirical loss in policy search. |

## Robustness frameworks recognize scenario specification as a determinant of robustness

| Citation | Precise summary | Support for this study |
|---|---|---|
| Herman et al. (2015) | Organize robustness frameworks along four choices, how alternatives are generated, how states of the world are sampled, which robustness measure is computed, and how controlling uncertainties are identified, and show on a regional water-supply case that these choices select substantially different alternatives. They define satisficing measures through Starr's domain criterion, the fraction of sampled states of the world meeting performance thresholds, and distinguish regret from a baseline state of the world from Savage regret relative to the best alternative in each state. | Places the search ensemble and the re-evaluation ensemble as instances of the sampling axis, justifies the satisficing robustness used on the re-evaluation ensemble, and its warning that too narrow a sample hides vulnerabilities motivates stressing the search ensemble toward hazards. The sampling effect is argued in the paper, not quantified. |
| McPhail et al. (2018) | Express any robustness metric as three transformations of the performance-over-scenarios matrix, a transformation of performance values, a selection of the scenario subset the metric uses, and the metric calculation itself, and show that the scenario subset a metric draws on governs its inherent risk aversion. | Makes explicit that robustness is defined on a performance-over-scenarios matrix, so the scenario set is upstream of every metric. Their scenario subset selection refers to which members a metric uses, not to ensemble construction. |
| McPhail et al. (2020) | Re-evaluated 4,611 lake-problem alternatives, all optimized under one reference scenario, over 900 scenario sets of 20 to 100 members that covered the deep-uncertainty space uniformly, in clustered diverse futures, or in a targeted worst-to-best spread. Robustness values under ten metrics generally differed by more than ten percent between set types, yet Kendall rankings of the alternatives were largely unchanged except for percentile-based metrics, and set size had little effect. | Supports using a single common re-evaluation ensemble as the only basis for cross-design comparison and warns that absolute robustness values are ensemble-specific. It concerns re-evaluation sets, not search ensembles. |
| Kasprzyk et al. (2013) | Define MORDM as four steps, problem formulation under the XLRM framing, generating alternatives with a many-objective evolutionary algorithm under a single baseline state of the world with well-characterized uncertainty, uncertainty analysis of those alternatives across 10,000 sampled deeply uncertain states of the world, and scenario discovery of the factor combinations that cause failure, and defer to future work the reapplication of search to the vulnerabilities scenario discovery reveals. | Fixes the reference point that MORDM search evaluates alternatives under a baseline state of the world, the assumption the scenario-design comparison relaxes, and names the extension this study pursues. |
| Quinn et al. (2017b) | Frame the lake problem as a stylized model of a socio-ecological system with a deeply uncertain tipping point, optimize direct policy search rules over 100 stochastic realizations of natural phosphorus inflows with objectives averaged across the realizations, and re-evaluate the resulting policies over 1,000 Latin hypercube states of the world with a domain-criterion robustness measure. They argue that optimizing directly to the deeply uncertain states of the world could yield large regrets if the extremes never occur. | Precedent for evaluating each candidate over a fixed stochastic ensemble during search and defining objectives as statistics over that ensemble, and a direct statement of the regret concern that this study measures as the price of hazard-stressed search. Describe the lake problem as a "stylized model of a socio-ecological system", never as a "coupled human-natural system". |
| Bonham et al. (2024) | Subsampled a 500-scenario Colorado River ensemble (streamflow, demand, and initial storage combined by conditioned Latin hypercube sampling) into ensembles of 50 to 450 scenarios and compared the resulting Lake Mead policy rankings with the full ensemble by Kendall rank correlation. Satisficing-type metrics ranked policies accurately with 50 to 300 scenarios, a 90 percent regret-from-best metric needed 400 or more, and a vulnerability metric needed 400 to 500 (verified against the full text on 2026-09-28). | Justifies the 500-SOW re-evaluation ensemble and the statement that satisficing measures need fewer SOWs than regret for a stable ranking. Their regret is measured from the best alternative, not from a status-quo policy. |
| Giuliani et al. (2021) | Describe direct policy search objectives as time aggregates filtered over K realizations of the hydrologic disturbance by an operator such as the expected value or the worst case, warn that expected-value aggregation can yield policies that perform poorly under extreme flow quantiles, and identify overfitting of policy parameters to the particular realizations used in simulation-based optimization as a key limitation to be addressed by out-of-sample testing on held-out records or synthetic ensembles. | Authoritative statement of the ensemble-evaluation convention, of the overfitting risk that motivates re-evaluating every design on a common ensemble, and of the neglect of extremes by averaged objectives that the HF design addresses at the ensemble level. |

## Overfitting and out-of-sample failure

| Citation | Precise summary | Support for this study |
|---|---|---|
| Brodeur et al. (2020) | Document the tendency of reservoir policy search to overfit the sequence of floods and droughts in a training record, showing at Folsom that policies selected on calibration performance alone overfit the timing of refill to the timing of floods, and that validation on bootstrapped paleo-conditioned sequences was the effective remedy. | Motivates out-of-sample evaluation of every design. Their own result is that validation-based selection, not bootstrapped training data, improved out-of-sample performance, so do not cite them as showing that ensemble training alone suffices. |
| Taylor et al. (2024) | Found under leave-one-year-out cross-validation that forecast-informed policies trained on a ten-year record of historical forecast ensembles fail at 3 of 14 reservoirs to hold releases below the safe maximum when confronted with larger floods absent from training, and recommend synthetic ensembles for training as the remedy. | Evidence that flood hazards missing from the training set produce out-of-sample failure, supporting inclusion of a flood dimension in the hazard space. Restrict the claim to floods. |
| Leitman et al. (2024) | Ran the current ACF Basin operating rules under 100 stationary stochastic realizations conditioned on the 1939 to 2012 record and found every performance metric less favorable than under the historical sequence, with drought operations in effect 27.8 percent of the time on average against 17.4 percent historically and emergency drought operations triggered where they never had been, and concluded that rules developed on historical hydrology alone were unacceptable. | Shows that rules acceptable on the observed record degrade under plausible stationary realizations, motivating the MC design as the minimal alternative to the HIST design. Do not write "equally plausible". |
| Cohen et al. (2021) | Attribute the difficulty of maintaining out-of-sample performance for reservoir control policies primarily to infrequent extreme events, which create a risk of overfitting to the training set. | Ties out-of-sample failure specifically to extremes, the conditions the HF design targets. |

## Scenario selection and ensemble construction methods

| Citation | Precise summary | Support for this study |
|---|---|---|
| Zatarain Salazar et al. (2017) | Built search ensembles of 50, 500, and 1,000 synthetic one-year realizations for the Conowingo system by Latin hypercube sampling over the empirical distribution of annual flow with the wettest and driest realizations always included, optimized worst-case objectives over each, and re-evaluated the resulting trade-offs on an independent 1,000-member set. Smaller ensembles produced noisier evaluations, ranking errors that can eliminate solutions during search, and re-evaluated losses of up to ten percent in hydropower revenue and cooling-water reliability, whereas the 1,000-member solutions were stable. | Direct precedent for quantile-stratified ensemble construction on one annual statistic and for out-of-sample verification of MOEA trade-offs. |
| Cohen et al. (2021) | Clustered 97 downscaled CMIP5 projections for a northern California reservoir system by end-of-century streamflow, snowpack, and the regret of the baseline policy relative to perfect foresight, trained multi-objective operating policies on seven training sets, and scored each on held-out projections with a regret-normalized hypervolume. Training on the 13 high-regret projections matched or exceeded training on all 50 available projections in both wet and dry test sets at roughly one third of the computing hours, which include the perfect-foresight optimizations needed to compute regret. | Closest precedent for constructing a search ensemble by scenario properties and for the hypothesis that stressing scenarios matter more than count. Their criterion depends on system response, whereas this study characterizes hazards from the streamflow sequences alone. |
| Herman et al. (2016) | Generate multi-site synthetic streamflows with a weighted bootstrap that samples the lowest p quantile of weekly flows n times more heavily than the record, so that drought frequency and severity can be pushed beyond historical experience for bottom-up vulnerability assessment without assigning likelihoods to the resulting scenarios. | Precedent for deliberately over-representing drought hazard relative to the generator's native frequency. The authors note that floods would need a different approach. |
| Zaniolo et al. (2024) | Present a synthetic weather generator that controls drought frequency, intensity, and duration in the generated sequences. | Generation-time control of drought properties, which requires the properties of interest to be specified before generation. |
| Salehabadi et al. (2024) | Evaluate 21 Colorado River streamflow ensembles entirely through statistics of the annual flow traces, including distributional moments, autocorrelation, run-theory drought length, cumulative deficit, intensity, and interarrival time, duration-severity curves, counts of years below and above thresholds, and an abstract storage-yield-reliability measure, and cluster the ensembles on these metrics. | Precedent for describing an ensemble by hazard statistics of its members independently of any reservoir simulation. Their high-flow metric is an annual count above threshold, not a daily flood pulse statistic. |
| Hilbers et al. (2019); Giudici et al. (2020) | Select small decision-relevant scenario subsets by importance scores derived from a provisional system design and by active learning on a robustness measure, respectively. | Both depend on the system's response to each candidate scenario, so they are specific to one problem formulation and their cost grows with the number of candidate scenarios examined. |

## Space-filling design criteria, scenario reduction, and change of measure

| Citation | Precise summary | Support for this study |
|---|---|---|
| Johnson, Moore and Ylvisaker (1990), UNVERIFIED (abstract only; the definitions are taken from Pronzato and Müller, 2012, p. 682, and the covering and packing reading from Pronzato, 2017, pp. 8 and 11, who attribute both criteria to it) | "Beginning with an arbitrary set and a distance defined on it, we develop the notions of minimax and maximin distance sets (designs)" for site selection under a prior on the surface (abstract, verified verbatim on 2026-09-28). The minimax-distance design minimizes the largest distance from any point of the region to its nearest design point, so that the design's balls of that radius cover the region, and the maximin-distance design maximizes the smallest distance between design points, the sphere-packing problem. | The two named criteria behind the coverage and diversity metrics of the HF design, the minimax distance relative to the candidate ensemble and the closest pair as the minimum edge of the minimum spanning tree. |
| Pronzato and Müller (2012) | Review space-filling designs for computer experiments around two geometric criteria, the minimax distance ("cover X with n balls of minimum radius") and the maximin distance, with discrepancy as the statistical uniformity measure, whose one-dimensional form "is just the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test statistic"; note that the minimax criterion is usually evaluated on a discretization of the region, that plain Latin hypercube designs guarantee only marginal stratification, and that Franco et al. (2009) characterize design classes by the empirical mean and variance of minimum-spanning-tree edge lengths (verified against the full text on 2026-09-28, pp. 682 to 689). | Source for the names and definitions of the HF design metrics, and for computing the minimax distance on the candidate ensemble itself as the discretization of the attainable region. |
| McKay, Beckman and Conover (1979) | Define Latin hypercube sampling as dividing the range of each input into N strata of equal marginal probability and sampling once from each, so that every input has "all portions of its distribution represented," a property that holds "no matter which components might turn out to be important," and prove the estimator unbiased with variance no larger than simple random sampling under monotonicity (verified against the full text on 2026-09-28, pp. 56 and 59 to 61). | The per-axis stratification property that makes the Kolmogorov-Smirnov distance to uniform the per-axis coverage statistic, which the exact Latin hypercube holds at or below 1/N on every axis. |
| Minasny and McBratney (2006) | Select a sample from a finite set of sites with ancillary data so that the sample forms "a Latin hypercube, or the multivariate distribution of X is maximally stratified," by simulated annealing on an objective that sums the deviations of each marginal stratum's count from one, the deviation of categorical proportions, and the deviation of the sample correlation matrix from the population's; note that "the solution given by the cLHS algorithm may not be an exact Latin hypercube, as the real data might not contain a combination that will give a true Latin hypercube" (verified against the full text on 2026-09-28, pp. 1381 to 1385). | The precedent for selecting real members to approximate a Latin hypercube design, and the source of the statement that an exact Latin hypercube may be unattainable on a finite candidate set, which is why the HF design measures its target displacement. |
| Franco, Vasseur, Corre and Sergent (2009), UNVERIFIED (abstract only; the preference for large mean edge length is characterized from Damblin et al., 2013, p. 12) | Propose a graph-based assessment of computer-experiment designs, "called Minimum Spanning Tree, [which] allows us to deduce two parameters, the mean and the standard deviation of the edges length, which characterize the points distribution" (abstract, verified verbatim on 2026-09-28); designs with large mean and small standard deviation are the quasi-periodic, well-spread class. | The diversity metric of the HF design, the mean minimum-spanning-tree edge length, reported against random subsets of the same size. |
| Damblin, Couplet and Iooss (2013) | Compare space-filling criteria for Latin hypercube designs (discrepancies, maximin distance, minimum-spanning-tree statistics) and find the minimum-spanning-tree statistics a "much more robust diagnosis than the mindist criterion," because the mean and standard deviation of the edge lengths are global characteristics whereas the minimum distance depends on a single pair; note that the minimax criterion is impracticable beyond three dimensions on a continuous region (verified against the full text on 2026-09-28, pp. 11 to 13 of the preprint). | Justifies reporting the edge-length distribution rather than the closest pair alone, and computing the minimax distance on the finite candidate ensemble, where it is a nearest-neighbour query. |
| Bonham et al. (2024) | Score subsampled scenario ensembles with three space-filling metrics from the DiceDesign package, the minimum pairwise distance and the mean and standard deviation of the minimum-spanning-tree edge lengths, scaled relative to the full set because each "decreases with sample size," and find that the mean edge length predicts the accuracy of robustness rankings best (R² 0.27 to 0.91), while noting that their results rest on subsampling 50 to 450 of 500 scenarios and may not hold when "100 scenarios were sampled from one million" (verified against the full text on 2026-09-28, pp. 5 to 9). | The water-resources precedent for the minimum-spanning-tree metric and for scaling size-dependent metrics against a reference; this study's reference is random subsets of the same size rather than the full set. |
| Dupačová, Gröwe-Kuska and Römisch (2003) | Pose scenario reduction as choosing a subset of prescribed cardinality and a redistributed probability measure that minimize a Kantorovich (Fortet-Mourier) distance to the original distribution, and prove that for a fixed retained subset the optimal redistribution gives each deleted scenario's probability to its nearest kept scenario, so that "the new probability of a kept scenario is equal to the sum of its former probability and of all probabilities of deleted scenarios that are closest to it" (Theorem 2; verified against the full text on 2026-09-28, pp. 499 to 501). | The nearest-member redistribution that states which candidate mass each HF member represents, and the identity between the mean candidate-to-nearest-member distance and the Kantorovich distance to the closest measure supported on the ensemble. |
| Heitsch and Römisch (2003) | State that "the problem of optimal scenario reduction consists in determining a probability measure that is supported by a subset of supp P of prescribed cardinality and is closest to P in terms of such a probability metric," restate the optimal redistribution rule, under which "the new probability of a preserved scenario is equal to the sum of its former probability and of all probabilities of deleted scenarios that are closest to it," and present the fast forward selection and simultaneous backward reduction heuristics (verified against the author preprint on 2026-09-28, pp. 1 to 5 of the preprint). | Companion citation for the redistribution rule and the source of the forward and backward heuristics. |
| Rujeerapaiboon, Schindler, Kuhn and Wiesemann (2022) | Revisit scenario reduction under the type-l Wasserstein distance, show that discrete reduction with l = 1 "represents a metric k-median problem," and recover the reduced distribution of Dupačová et al.'s algorithm by giving each support point the fraction of original scenarios closest to it (verified against the full text on 2026-09-28, pp. 210 and 228). | Confirms the nearest-member redistribution as the standard reduced measure and names the optimization (k-median) that a mean-distance-optimal selection would solve. |
| Owen (2013); Homem-de-Mello and Bayraksan (2014) | Owen states the importance-sampling identity that the expectation under the nominal density equals the expectation under the sampling density of the integrand times "the likelihood ratio" p/q, and defines the effective sample size n_e = (Σ w_i)² / Σ w_i², attributed to Kong (1992); Homem-de-Mello and Bayraksan give the same identity with the likelihood ratio L(ξ) = f(ξ)/q(ξ), note that both estimators are unbiased, and that a poorly chosen sampling density "might lead to an increase in variance" (Owen verified against the online text on 2026-09-28, ch. 9, Eqs. 9.1 and 9.13; Homem-de-Mello and Bayraksan against the full text, §7.4). | The likelihood-ratio reading of N w_i and the effective-sample-size statistic that quantifies the variance cost of reading HF frequencies as generator-measure probabilities; the study reports the statistic and does not reweight. |
| Kish (1965, 1992) | Kish (1992) gives the variance inflation of unequal weights explicitly as "(1 + L) = nΣk_j² / (Σk_j)²" (Eq. 4.3, p. 191), notes that L "may be viewed as the relvariance cv² ... of the relative weights," and defines "the effective sample size n_d = n/Deff" (p. 192), citing Section 11.7 of Kish (1965) for the derivation (Kish 1992 verified against the journal scan on 2026-09-28; the 1965 book was not accessed and is cited through Kish's own cross-reference). | Attribution for the effective sample size (Σ w)² / Σ w². |
| Kuhn (1955) | Poses the assignment problem as "the quest for an assignment of persons to jobs so that the sum of the n scores so obtained is as large as possible" and solves it exactly "by solving a finite sequence of associated Simple Assignment Problems" (verified against the full text on 2026-09-28, pp. 83 and 89). The rectangular case with fewer targets than candidates is the implementation of Crouse (2016) used by scipy. | Names the problem of which the HF construction's sequential rule is a heuristic; the exact assignment is computed on a k-nearest-candidate graph with a linear-programming duality certificate. |
| McSweeney and Jones (2016), UNVERIFIED (abstract only) | Assess how representative the five-model CMIP5 subset of ISI-MIP is of the full ensemble by "the fraction of the full range of future projections captured across different regions and seasons," which "varies from 0.5 to 0.9 for temperature (median 0.75) and 0.3 to 0.8 for precipitation (median 0.55)" (abstract, verified verbatim on 2026-09-28). | Precedent for the per-axis span fraction as the range metric. |

## Hazard framing and hazard metrics

| Citation | Precise summary | Support for this study |
|---|---|---|
| Simpson et al. (2021) | Adopt the IPCC framing in which hazard, exposure, and vulnerability are the determinants of climate risk, define risk as the potential for adverse consequences for human or ecological systems, treat hazard as a determinant composed of physical drivers such as temperature and heavy precipitation, and add response as a fourth determinant whose interactions can amplify or dampen risk. | Supplies the vocabulary for describing droughts and floods in a streamflow realization as hazard drivers distinct from the exposure and vulnerability that the reservoir system and its objectives supply. They give no standalone hazard definition, so cite them for terminology, not for independence from the system. |
| Hadjimichael et al. (2020); AghaKouchak et al. (2021) | Characterize hydrologic hazards with streamflow-based measures so that the operational consequences of a hazard are resolved by simulation of the managed system rather than embedded in the hazard measure (the framing adopted in the sibling stochastic DRB manuscript). | Grounds the study's motivating property, that hazard characteristics are computed on the streamflow sequence alone, independently of the system's operations, response, or outcomes. |
| Vicente-Serrano et al. (2012); Tijdeman et al. (2020) | Define and evaluate the Standardized Streamflow Index, including the six-month window, with per-month distribution fitting. | Basis for the SSI-6 drought characterization. |
| Parry, Wilby, Prudhomme and Wood (2016) | Define drought termination "as a phase of a drought, rather than an instantaneous point in time," divide events "at the point of the maximum negative flow anomaly (Bravar and Kavvas, 1991) into two phases: drought development and drought termination," and define the termination duration as "the number of months between" the start and end of the termination phase and the termination rate as "the difference between the drought magnitude and the termination magnitude, divided by the drought termination duration," reported in anomaly units per month (verified against the full text on 2026-09-28, §3.1, pp. 4267 to 4268). | The definition of the study's development rate and termination rate, the severity divided by the elapsed duration of each phase, with the two phases delimited at the SSI-6 minimum. |
| Parry, Prudhomme, Wilby and Wood (2016) | Propose drought termination as "a period between the maximum negative anomaly and a return to above-average conditions," after which "the duration, rate and seasonality of drought termination can be derived" (abstract verified verbatim on 2026-09-28; 74 citations). | The concept behind both phase rates, and the source for ending the termination phase at the return of the index above the threshold. |
| Wu et al. (2024), UNVERIFIED (citation level) | Characterize streamflow droughts by onset, maximum deficit, and termination, splitting each event into an intensification stage from onset to the maximum deficit and a recovery stage from the maximum deficit to termination. | Confirms the two-phase division for streamflow drought specifically. |
| Otkin et al. (2018), UNVERIFIED (abstract only) | Propose that flash drought be defined by "its rate of intensification rather than its duration," with droughts that develop much more rapidly than normal identified as flash droughts. | Names the rate-of-intensification facet that the development rate measures. |
| Fleig et al. (2006); Van Loon (2015) | Threshold-based identification of streamflow drought events, with retention thresholds and termination requirements that pool closely spaced deficits. | Basis for the drought event definition. |
| Brunner (2023); Brunner and Gilleland (2020) | Brunner (2023) defines flood event samples by block maxima or peaks over threshold and characterizes each flood by peak discharge, volume, and duration. Brunner and Gilleland (2020) evaluate a stochastic streamflow generator on flows above the 95th percentile and on peak-over-threshold flood occurrences and argue that hazard assessment should extend beyond peak magnitude to event duration and volume. | Precedent for the peaks-over-threshold flood metrics (peak discharge and pulse duration above the historical 95th percentile) applied to stochastic streamflow ensembles. Neither paper states a flood threshold percentile (UNVERIFIED). |

## The DRB test case

| Citation | Precise summary | Support for this study |
|---|---|---|
| Kolesar and Serio (2011) | Used OASIS simulation on the 1928 to 2000 record, designed experiments with regression response surfaces, and habitat analysis to develop storage-zone feedback release rules for the three NYC Delaware reservoirs under a drought-day neutrality constraint imposed by the Decree Parties. The DRBC adopted their adaptive release framework as the 2007 FFMP with the release quantities of the New York State Department of Environmental Conservation, which matched the authors' proposal on 98 of 105 matrix entries and were revised toward the authors' values in 2009, and the authors estimate the rules raise critical summer fish habitat by about 200 percent for a 3 percent increase in NYC drought risk. | Documents that the FFMP's structure was designed by simulation against the historical record under a single drought-risk constraint and a full-diversion assumption, the design basis this study revisits with many objectives and non-historical ensembles. Never write that the rules were shown to carry "no increase" in drought risk. |
| Hogarty (1970), UNVERIFIED (scanned PDF without a text layer) | The 1961 to 1967 drought of record led NYC to cease downstream releases in June 1965 and the DRBC to declare its first drought emergency in July 1965, modifying the 1954 Decree with the unanimous consent of the Decree Parties to reduce NYC's diversion and cut the Montague flow objective to 1,200 cfs, until the emergency ended in March 1967 after 20 months (facts per the DRBC's 2019 drought overview, which cites Hogarty). | Establishes that the basin's institutional drought rules were forged under a drought more extreme than the record later used for design. Zotero dates the report 1969 and the DRBC library copy 1970. Confirm the year and case number, or OCR the PDF, before publication. |

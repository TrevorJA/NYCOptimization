## 1. Introduction

*[Held as a structured outline until the Results are complete. Each numbered entry corresponds to one planned paragraph. The contribution statement and research questions are drafted because the Methodology depends on them. All other paragraphs are outlined only, to be written against the final Results.]*

- **P1. The Delaware River Basin stakes.** Open with the decision problem, not the method, so that the re-optimization question is motivated before the methodological one. The three NYC-operated headwater reservoirs supply roughly half of the city's drinking water while sustaining mandated flows that protect downstream states and hold the salt front below Philadelphia's water supply intakes. The operating rules balancing these obligations, most recently the 2017 FFMP, are negotiated under a unanimity requirement among the five Decree parties and revisited on multi-year renegotiation cycles. Those rules were tuned during a comparatively wet epoch: the 1960s drought of record predates the basin's persistent post-1970 pluvial, and paleoclimate reconstructions indicate the basin can produce droughts at least as severe (Pederson et al., 2013; Devineni et al., 2013 — to be imported to the collection). Whether renegotiation-scale adjustments of the FFMP's negotiated constants could improve outcomes for multiple parties simultaneously is an institutionally live question, and it motivates the second research question directly.

- **P2. Many-objective search and the evaluation ensemble.** Simulation-based many-objective evolutionary search is the established framework for exploring operating-rule adaptation and revealing trade-offs among competing water uses (Reed et al., 2013; Giuliani et al., 2016; Herman et al., 2020). Introduce the scenario-design challenge with care: the search ensemble is fixed before search and reused across every one of the hundreds of thousands of candidate-policy evaluations, so its composition is the lens through which the algorithm sees every policy it considers, and the hydrologic conditions it contains determine which stresses shape the retained policies. In most applications the search ensemble is inherited rather than designed, with two prevailing constructions, the historical record and an independent sample from a fitted stochastic generator, and neither is chosen for the stress content it presents to the search.

- **P3. The evidence that composition matters is real and two-sided.** Supporting evidence: the properties of training scenarios determine out-of-sample robustness, with high-regret subsets matching full-ensemble performance at roughly one third of the cost (Cohen et al., 2021), the drought type embedded in a training ensemble changes the infrastructure installed and the state variables a policy consults (Zaniolo et al., 2023), and policies calibrated to a single sequence overfit its event ordering (Brodeur et al., 2020). Cautionary evidence: scenarios selected for policy relevance and diversity did not outperform an arbitrary set (Eker and Kwakkel, 2018), scenario selection moves robustness values far more than robustness rankings (McPhail et al., 2020), and policies designed under one across-scenario aggregation and scored under another are systematically dominated by policies designed under the scoring measure (Giuliani and Castelletti, 2016), a direct caution for any method that alters the scenario distribution during search. Close with the design consequence: the question is open, and answering it requires an experiment built to detect the effect if it exists and to report a bounded, informative null if it does not.

- **P4. Three spaces.** Distinguish the input space (generator parameters, climate multipliers, and demand factors, where a sampled point is one of the states-of-the-world, SOWs, of the robust-decision-making lineage: Kasprzyk et al., 2013; Trindade et al., 2017), the outcome space (simulation outputs, the objective values of a policy under a scenario), and, between them, the hazard space, the space of hydrologic hazard metrics computed directly on each realized streamflow sequence before any system simulation. Hazard coordinates are properties of the scenario rather than of the simulated response, and they are computable for millions of candidate sequences at negligible cost. One sentence on the nearest existing construct, the exposure space of the scenario-neutral tradition (Culley et al., 2016; Fowler et al., 2024): exposure spaces are attribute targets imposed on generator inputs, whereas hazard coordinates are measured on realized sequences. Keep this paragraph lean and defer the full taxonomy to Section 3.2.3.

- **P5. The gap, organized by the dependency each prior approach carries.** A probability-faithful generator sample populates the common center of the hazard distribution densely and the severe corners sparsely, although the severe corners are where reservoir policies are decided. Prior constructions that control hazard content either preserve the generating probability (one-dimensional stratification of a stationary population: Zatarain Salazar et al., 2017), commit to a few discrete analyst-chosen event types at generation time (Herman et al., 2016; Zaniolo et al., 2023, 2024), or require simulating or optimizing every candidate scenario through the system model (Hilbers et al., 2019; Giudici et al., 2020; and the baseline-regret selection of Cohen et al., 2021, which requires one perfect-foresight optimization per candidate). Space-filling subsampling of SOW ensembles exists for post-hoc robustness ranking rather than for search (Bonham et al., 2024), small carried ensembles materially change the solutions found (Watson and Kasprzyk, 2017), and vulnerability-plus-diversity selection has been applied outside the search loop (Carlsen et al., 2016). Close with the open problem as Bartholomew and Kwakkel (2020) state it: "Research is needed into the selection [of] scenarios which as a set contain the appropriately stressing conditions against which solutions have to be robust, while also capturing the scenarios under which one would like to have near optimal performance."

**P6. Contribution statement (drafted).** This study contributes hazard filling, a search-ensemble construction that selects scenarios from a large candidate ensemble of synthetic streamflow sequences so that the retained members cover the hydrologic hazard space, deliberately over-representing severe hazard conditions relative to their frequency under the generating process. Because hazard coordinates are computed on the streamflow sequences alone, the selection is simulation-free, scales to candidate ensembles of order one million sequences, and transfers across objective formulations. The selection procedure is deliberately the simplest defensible space-filling design, placing Latin hypercube anchor points in the hazard space and assigning each anchor to its nearest unselected candidate sequence: it is deterministic given its anchors, has no tuning parameters, and its construction variability is therefore measurable under replication. We test this construction against the prevailing default, an independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) sample from the same stochastic generator, on the re-optimization of New York City's (NYC) Delaware River Basin reservoir operations within the Flexible Flow Management Program (FFMP) rule structure, simulated with the open-source Pywr-DRB model (Hamilton et al., 2024). To our knowledge, this is the first controlled comparison of hazard-space scenario selection against probabilistic sampling for many-objective policy search on a real water system. The comparison holds ensemble size, record length, and the function-evaluation budget fixed, replicates the entire ensemble construction of each design, and evaluates both designs' policies on one common deeply uncertain re-evaluation ensemble (Section 3).

**P7. Research questions (drafted).** We use this experimental design to address two research questions. First, does constructing the search ensemble by space-filling coverage of the hazard space, rather than by independent sampling from the same stochastic generator, change the robustness of the resulting Pareto-approximate policies under deeply uncertain re-evaluation? Second, do re-optimized FFMP policies exist that simultaneously improve multiple stakeholder outcomes, spanning water supply reliability, Montague and Trenton flow-target compliance, downstream flood exposure, and minimum reservoir storage, without degrading others below current performance on the same re-evaluation ensemble?

**P8. Roadmap (drafted).** The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 describes the Delaware River Basin test case and the Pywr-DRB simulation model. Section 3 details the methodology, comprising the many-objective problem formulation, the three scenario designs and the hazard-filling construction, the computational experiment, and the deep uncertainty re-evaluation and robustness analysis. Section 4 presents the results, Section 5 discusses their implications, and Section 6 concludes.

## 2. The Delaware River Basin Test Case

### 2.1 Institutional Water Management and the FFMP

The Delaware River Basin (DRB) spans New York, Pennsylvania, New Jersey, and Delaware and supplies approximately one half of NYC's drinking water through three upper-basin reservoirs (Cannonsville, Pepacton, and Neversink) whose diversions pass through the Rondout balancing reservoir and the Delaware Aqueduct. Basin operations are governed by the 1954 U.S. Supreme Court Decree in *New Jersey v. New York*, which sets the NYC diversion allocation at 800 million gallons per day (MGD), entitles New Jersey (NJ) to a 100 MGD diversion, and obligates NYC to make compensating reservoir releases that maintain a minimum flow of 1,750 cubic feet per second (cfs, equivalent to 1,131 MGD) on the Delaware River mainstem at Montague, NJ. The Office of the Delaware River Master within the U.S. Geological Survey administers these Decree provisions. A separate flow objective of 3,000 cfs (1,939 MGD) at Trenton, NJ, established under the Delaware River Basin Commission's Water Code and implemented through the basin's operating agreements, protects downstream ecosystems and limits upstream migration of the salt front toward the Philadelphia and Camden water supply intakes. Changes to the operation of NYC's Delaware reservoirs require unanimous consent of the five Decree parties.

Since 2007, NYC reservoir releases and diversions have been managed under successive versions of the Flexible Flow Management Program, most recently the 2017 FFMP. The FFMP is a staged, storage-indexed rule structure. Combined NYC storage is compared daily against seasonally varying threshold curves that partition storage into operating zones ranging from flood-mitigation levels through normal operations to three drought stages. Each zone prescribes diversion curtailments for NYC and NJ together with scaling factors on each reservoir's minimum release, modulated by seasonal release profiles, and additional flood-mitigation releases apply in the highest storage zones.

The system's operational tension was established empirically by the 1960s drought of record (1964-1967), during which storage could not simultaneously support the decreed NYC diversion and the Montague compensating releases, emergency negotiations among the Decree parties coupled diversion curtailments with flow-target reductions, and the salt front advanced to within approximately 13 km of Philadelphia's drinking water intakes (Hogarty, 1970; Kolesar and Serio, 2011). The storage-indexed drought rules that the FFMP carries forward descend from those emergency measures. Severe drought therefore forces NYC's authorized diversion, the Montague compensating releases, and downstream ecological flows into direct competition for the same depleted storage, while wet-period storage management trades flood mitigation against drought reserve. This governance structure supplies a natural many-objective formulation. The FFMP's parameterized rules, which consist of negotiated quantities such as release baselines, drought curtailment factors, and storage-zone boundaries, define a decision space whose re-optimization is institutionally meaningful because the rule structure is preserved and only its negotiated constants are varied (Section 3.1). Because any change to NYC's Delaware operations requires the unanimous consent of the five Decree parties, adaptations must remain legible as renegotiation-scale perturbations of the negotiated compromise, a constraint the decision space of Section 3.1.1 is designed to respect.

### 2.2 Pywr-DRB Simulation of Current Operations

All policy evaluations use Pywr-DRB, an open-source daily water-availability and drought-risk model of the basin built on the Pywr simulation framework (Hamilton et al., 2024). Pywr-DRB represents the four NYC reservoirs and the major lower-basin reservoirs, the FFMP rule structure with its storage zones, seasonal release profiles, drought curtailments, and flood-mitigation releases, the Montague and Trenton flow targets with their time-of-travel lags, and the NYC and NJ deliveries under the Decree's running-average accounting.

During optimization we use a trimmed configuration of the model in which the non-NYC reservoirs are simulated once under their default rules and their releases are supplied to the trimmed model as fixed boundary inflows, so that each function evaluation solves only the NYC-relevant portion of the network. This simplification is admissible because the decision variables (Section 3.1.1) alter only NYC operations and no lower-basin operating rule in the model consults an NYC state variable. Because the boundary releases are policy-independent, they are computed once per streamflow realization and reused for every policy evaluated on that realization. The deep uncertainty re-evaluation (Section 3.4) also uses the trimmed configuration, with a single boundary-release simulation pass computed per realization under that ensemble's perturbed forcing, so the boundary releases remain consistent with the forcing of every re-evaluation scenario. The full model is used only to generate the boundary releases and to simulate the status-quo baseline policy on the historical record. Verification that the trimmed configuration reproduces full-model objective values under matched inputs is reported in Text S1 in Supporting Information S1.

## 3. Methodology

Our experimental design addresses the two research questions posed in Section 1 by holding the stochastic generating process, the ensemble dimensions, the objective formulation, and the search algorithm fixed while varying only the rule that determines which streamflow realizations constitute the search ensemble. Figure 2 summarizes this design. We first formulate the re-optimization of NYC's reservoir operations as an eight-objective problem over 36 decision variables that parameterize the FFMP rule structure (Section 3.1). We then define three scenario designs for the search ensemble (Section 3.2): the historical record, an i.i.d. sample from a stationary stochastic generator, and the proposed hazard-filling construction, which selects realizations from a large candidate ensemble for space-filling coverage of a hydrologic hazard space. Section 3.3 describes the computational experiment, including the ensemble sizing, the replication scheme that separates ensemble-construction variability from search variability, and the Multi-Master Borg MOEA configuration. Finally, every Pareto-approximate policy from every search is re-evaluated on a single deeply uncertain re-evaluation ensemble, on which satisficing robustness metrics support all cross-design comparisons (Section 3.4).

### 3.1 Many-Objective Problem Formulation

We pose the re-optimization of NYC's reservoir operations as the many-objective problem

$$
\min_{\mathbf{x} \in \mathcal{X}} \; \mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x}; E) = \big(J_1(\mathbf{x}; E), \ldots, J_8(\mathbf{x}; E)\big)
\quad \text{s.t.} \quad c_{\mathrm{fld}}(\mathbf{x}) \le 0,
\tag{1}
$$

where $\mathbf{x}$ is the decision vector parameterizing the FFMP rule structure (Section 3.1.1), $E$ is the search ensemble of streamflow realizations presented to the search (Section 3.2), and $J_1, \ldots, J_8$ are the objectives aggregated over $E$ (Section 3.1.2 and Table 2). Maximization objectives are negated so that all objectives are posed to the algorithm in minimization form. The feasibility constraint $c_{\mathrm{fld}}$ is computed from decision-variable arithmetic alone, requiring ordered flood-zone releases (Section 3.1.1).

#### 3.1.1 FFMP Decision Variables

The decision vector parameterizes the 2017 FFMP rule structure with 36 variables,

$$
\mathbf{x} = \big[\, \boldsymbol{\delta}^{\mathrm{NYC}}, \; \boldsymbol{\delta}^{\mathrm{NJ}}, \; \mathbf{v}, \; \boldsymbol{\tau}, \; \mathbf{s}^{\mathrm{rel}}, \; \mathbf{s}^{\mathrm{tgt}}, \; \mathbf{s}^{\mathrm{fld}} \,\big],
\tag{2}
$$

where $\boldsymbol{\delta}^{\mathrm{NYC}}$ holds stage-wise reductions of the NYC diversion allocation for the three drought stages and $\boldsymbol{\delta}^{\mathrm{NJ}}$ holds the corresponding NJ allocation reductions for the two deepest stages (5 variables); each of these variables is the additional fractional reduction applied on entry to its drought stage, so the effective delivery factor at a stage is one minus the running sum of reductions. Further, $\mathbf{v}$ holds nine vertical shifts of the storage-zone threshold curves, $\boldsymbol{\tau}$ holds six temporal shifts that slide each curve along the day-of-year axis, $\mathbf{s}^{\mathrm{rel}}$ holds four seasonal scaling factors on the daily minimum-release profiles of the conservation zones, $\mathbf{s}^{\mathrm{tgt}}$ holds six scaling factors on the Montague and Trenton drought-stage flow-target factor tables (one per gauge and drought stage), and $\mathbf{s}^{\mathrm{fld}}$ holds six scaling factors on the flood-zone spill-mitigation release schedules (one per zone and reservoir). Table 1 lists every variable with its baseline value and bounds.

The zone-boundary adjustment is restricted to plateau shifts and one temporal shift per curve. Each threshold curve is a trapezoid over the year whose low storage plateau, the fall and winter void, carries its own vertical shift. The three drought-stage curves, whose high plateaus sit below capacity, carry an independent shift of the high plateau, the spring and summer refill target, so that void depth is decoupled from the refill target. The three flood-zone curves refill to full capacity and their high plateaus are held fixed, because the FFMP treats spring refill-to-full as an essential requirement, and a lowered refill target would constitute a permanent forfeit of effective storage capacity rather than a renegotiation-scale adjustment of the operating rules. The FFMP's negotiated flood-mitigation instrument, the seasonal void depth, remains fully searchable through the low-plateau shifts. This restriction keeps each adjustment legible to basin stakeholders, since every variable maps to a visible flat segment of a rule curve, and every curve retains its trapezoidal shape because the two ramps re-interpolate between the shifted plateaus.

The flood-release scaling factors are season-invariant by design, matching the FFMP, which holds these release schedules constant across its tables and seasons. Seasonal flood-mitigation policy instead enters through the zone-boundary shifts, mirroring the FFMP's own structure, in which seasonal flood mitigation is encoded in the storage-zone geometry rather than in the release rates. The seasonal minimum-release scaling factors use the FFMP's own season definitions (winter December through March, spring April and May, summer June through August, and fall September through November). Because the FFMP tables' finer date bins nest within these seasons, seasonally scaled schedules change value only on the FFMP's own bin boundaries.

Three quantity classes are held fixed rather than optimized, because each is anchored in the basin's legal framework rather than in the FFMP's negotiated operating constants. The 1954 Decree quantities, comprising the Montague flow target and the NYC diversion allocation, together with the Water Code's Trenton flow objective, are the legal anchors of the comparison, and varying them would pose a renegotiation of the Decree rather than of the FFMP. The reservoir minimum-release baselines are fixed because the FFMP itself never varies them, and operational variation enters through the forecast-based table selection that the seasonal scaling factors emulate. The FFMP's maximum combined discharge rates are physical and regulatory limits of the release works and spillways at which scheduled flood-zone releases are capped.

Bounds are set from the FFMP's own structure and from operational evidence, with the provenance of each bound class given in the note to Table 1. The NYC and NJ allocation reductions share one depth-preserving rule: every stage reduction has a lower bound of zero, and each upper bound is the negotiated FFMP stage increment plus headroom, with each party's total headroom allocated so that the maximum cumulative curtailment remains renegotiation-scale (delivery factors bottom out at 0.50 of the NYC allocation and 0.65 of the NJ allocation). The baselines reproduce the FFMP-negotiated curtailment schedule exactly because those values are the settled compromise among the Decree parties, so the search explores renegotiation-scale adjustments of the staged curtailment schedule rather than an unconstrained reallocation of deliveries; the zero lower bounds also admit policies that waive curtailment at a stage, leaving the value of curtailment to be measured by the reliability and storage objectives rather than imposed by the bounds. Because the reductions are non-negative increments, deeper drought stages can never permit larger diversions, so delivery-stage monotonicity holds by construction and requires no constraint. Feasibility of the remaining conditions is enforced at evaluation time by monotonicity clamps, so that storage-zone curves may not cross and flood-zone releases obey the zone ordering. The flood-ordering condition is additionally posed to the optimizer as the formal constraint of Equation 1; because it is computed from decision-variable arithmetic alone, infeasible vectors are discarded under constraint-dominance before any simulation is spent on them. The zone-boundary conditions remain clamp-only because the clamped geometry is itself the intended policy. The FFMP's structural logic, including its storage-indexed stages, seasonal profiles, and running-average delivery accounting, is preserved exactly.

#### 3.1.2 Objective Formulations and Ensemble Aggregation

The objective set spans the stakeholder priorities that the basin's governance encodes, comprising NYC supply, the flow obligations protecting New Jersey and Philadelphia, downstream flood exposure, and the protection of reservoir storage. Eight objectives are active (Table 2). The eighth, NJ delivery reliability, carries New Jersey's independent Decree interest. A redundancy screen over samples of constraint-feasible policies confirmed that it is not collinear with any other objective (Spearman $|\rho| \le 0.38$ everywhere, and $\le 0.08$ with Trenton flow reliability, Text S5 in Supporting Information S1).

All metrics are computed on the metric window of each simulation, defined as the daily output beginning six calendar months after the simulation start and delimited by date, with weekly accounting following the Decree convention. Each simulation begins from an assumed initial storage of 80 percent of capacity, the Pywr-DRB default initial condition lying within the reservoirs' normal operating range, and the excluded six months serve as an initialization period that attenuates the influence of this assumption. The same exclusion governs the hazard metrics used for scenario selection (Section 3.2.3), so selection and evaluation score the identical window.

Compliance is always scored against static regulatory targets, namely the 1954 Decree quantities of 800 MGD for NYC, 1,131.05 MGD at Montague, and 100 MGD for NJ, together with the Trenton equivalent-flow objective of 1,938.95 MGD, and never against the FFMP's storage-indexed drought-adjusted targets. Scoring against the storage-indexed targets would allow a policy to satisfy its obligations by triggering the drought curtailments that lower those obligations. For NYC and NJ supply, the Decree limits the running average of diversion rather than any single day's diversion, so daily demand is not clipped at the static allocation. Instead, each day's delivery target is the realizable entitlement, defined as the smaller of that day's demand and the running-average allowance accrued to it under the FFMP delivery accounting, reconstructed at the static allocation so that a policy's own drought curtailments cannot lower it. A demand peak above the flat daily allocation is honored whenever prior under-use has accrued the allowance for it, while demand beyond the accrued allowance is not counted as owed.

Two formulation choices follow the objective-stability findings of Quinn et al. (2017) and the convergence findings of Bonham et al. (2024). First, tail objectives use the conditional value-at-risk at the 90 percent level (CVaR90), defined as the mean of the worst decile of weekly deficits (Rockafellar and Uryasev, 2000; Löhndorf, 2016; Fairbrother et al., 2022), rather than the single worst week, which Quinn et al. (2017) identify as a high-variance, low-information signal. Second, flood exposure is measured as downstream flood exceedance, the mean annual sum over days of the largest excess, across three vulnerable reservoir-tailwater gauges (Hale Eddy, Fishs Eddy, and Bridgeville), of river stage above the National Weather Service minor flood stage, in units of ft-days per year and normalized per metric-window year so that the metric is invariant to record length. This magnitude-weighted form resolves policy differences that a day count collapses onto a few integer values, tracks observed annual flood magnitude, and avoids the known pathologies of expected-damage objectives (Quinn et al., 2017). Flood-day counts at the NWS minor and major stages and at the FFMP action stage are retained as reported diagnostics. The NYC minimum-storage objective uses the fifth percentile of daily aggregate storage rather than the single-day minimum, the same tail-stability logic. Trenton flow reliability stands in for salt-front position because the Trenton flow objective exists to repel the salt front, which makes the two physically redundant, and because the flow form avoids reliance on a statistical salinity emulator in the extreme droughts where objective values are decided.

For the single-realization Historical design (Section 3.2.1), the temporal metrics of Table 2 are the objectives. Each ensemble design must additionally collapse a matrix of objective values across realizations to one scalar per objective at each function evaluation. Following the aggregation formalism of Quinn et al. (2017) and Zatarain Salazar et al. (2017), each objective is expressed as the composition of a within-ensemble temporal aggregator $\Phi$ and an across-ensemble noise filter $\Psi$,

$$
J_d \;=\; \Psi_{(r,y)}\Big[\, \Phi_{t \in y}\big[\, g_d(t, r) \,\big] \,\Big],
\qquad r = 1, \ldots, N, \;\; y = 1, \ldots, L-1,
\tag{3}
$$

where $g_d(t, r)$ is the daily value underlying objective $d$ on day $t$ of realization $r$, $\Phi$ aggregates within each whole water-year unit $y$ of the metric window to produce an annual metric per realization-year, and $\Psi$ filters across the $N(L-1)$ annual units. Realization windows are October-aligned, so the metric window opens on April 1 of the first water year, the first whole water-year unit is the second water year, and each realization therefore contributes $L - 1$ rather than $L$ annual units in Equation 3. The per-objective instantiations of $\Phi$ and $\Psi$ are listed in Table 2.

The reliability objectives take $\Phi$ as an annual failure indicator, which registers a failure when at least $k$ weeks in the annual unit violate the corresponding target, and $\Psi$ as the frequency of non-failure years. This satisficing-frequency form is the search-phase reliability operator used throughout the regional water-supply planning literature (Zeff et al., 2014; Trindade et al., 2017; Gold et al., 2023). The failure-week counts were screened for saturation across both campaign ensemble compositions (Text S5 in Supporting Information S1). Trenton and NJ use $k = 1$ because larger counts saturate the Trenton ranking, and NYC delivery and Montague use $k = 3$. The deficit objectives take $\Phi$ as the within-year CVaR90 deficit, the storage objective takes $\Phi$ as the annual minimum of daily aggregate storage, and all three take $\Psi$ as the worst first-percentile annual unit, the stable tail form of Quinn et al. (2017, 2018). The flood-exceedance objective takes $\Psi$ as the mean across annual units, corresponding to expected annual exceedance. A 99th-percentile variant was screened and retained as a diagnostic because at the campaign's aggregate annual-unit count it collapses onto a few discrete values and is an order of magnitude noisier under bootstrap resampling (Text S5 in Supporting Information S1).

The historical record is scored as consecutive annual units with inherited storage state, following Quinn et al. (2018), so it carries the same annual-unit denominator per realization-year as the ensemble designs (76 metric-bearing annual units on its 77-year record, where unit boundaries are scoring windows rather than simulation restarts). Annual units within a realization are serially dependent, since consecutive failure-years are precisely how multi-year droughts express themselves in this scheme, so the effective sample size is below $N(L-1)$. At the campaign ensemble size ($N = 100$ realizations of length $L = 10$ years) the aggregate denominator is 900 annual units, comparable in count to the 1,000 one-year realizations that Quinn et al. (2017, 2018) use to stabilize the same worst-first-percentile operator. Because serial dependence attenuates the information content of these units, the equivalence is quantified rather than asserted, through a block-bootstrap estimator-stability analysis at the realization level reported with the calibration diagnostics (Text S5 in Supporting Information S1; these diagnostics are being finalized). This equivalence is the basis for a per-evaluation ensemble smaller than the 1,000-realization convention of the regional water-supply planning literature (Section 3.3.1).

Two properties of this aggregation scheme carry the design comparison. First, the temporal metrics of Table 2 and the entire re-evaluation metric set (Section 3.4.2) are identical across designs. Only the across-realization stage differs, and it differs only because the scenario designs present different ensemble compositions to it. Second, cross-design commensurability during search is neither required nor claimed. A design that deliberately over-represents severe hazard conditions computes its search objectives under a scenario distribution that differs from the generating process (Section 3.2.4), and the common re-evaluation ensemble of Section 3.4 is the only point of cross-design comparison.

### 3.2 Scenario Designs for the Search Ensemble

#### 3.2.1 Definition of the Three Scenario Designs

We compare three constructions of the search ensemble, all drawn from a single stationary population and all evaluated at matched ensemble size and record length except where a design cannot be matched by construction (Table 3). A survey of search-ensemble constructions in the water resources policy-search literature is provided in Text S11 in Supporting Information S1.

The Historical design (HIST) is the observed record simulated as one continuous realization of 77 water years (October 1945 through September 2022). It is the reference for prevailing applied practice (Giuliani et al., 2016; Herman et al., 2020). Because it cannot be matched in ensemble size or function-evaluation budget, it is reported as a reference rather than entered into the controlled contrast, and because its single realization resolves the reliability objectives below their own sampling noise (Table 2 note), its reliability comparisons are read as illustrative.

The Probabilistic Sampling design (PS) consists of $N$ realizations of length $L$ years drawn i.i.d. from the stationary stochastic generator (Section 3.2.2) and held fixed for the entire search. This is the standard ensemble construction of the regional water-supply planning literature (Quinn et al., 2017; Zatarain Salazar et al., 2017) and the reference against which designed selection is judged.

The Hazard Filling design (HF) is the proposed construction. It selects $N$ realizations of length $L$ years from a large candidate ensemble by space-filling coverage of the hydrologic hazard space, using the selection procedure of Section 3.2.4. It is constructed from the same stationary generator as PS at the same $N$ and $L$, so the two designs differ only in the rule that determines which realizations enter the search ensemble, the exact-control property established in Section 3.2.2. The contrast between PS and HF is the direct test of the first research question.

The stationary population underlying all three designs is the flow model of the prevailing water-supply optimization literature (Herman et al., 2014; Trindade et al., 2017; Zatarain Salazar et al., 2017; Gold et al., 2023). Hazard variation within it arises entirely from natural variability, in that some 10-year realizations contain severe multi-year droughts and most do not. Restricting the search population in this way is deliberate. It isolates the first research question, which concerns the rule that selects realizations from a fixed generating process, without confounding that rule with a change in the generating process itself. The deeply uncertain climate-forcing space enters the study only through the re-evaluation ensemble (Section 3.4.1), which makes the re-evaluation a test of generalization to conditions absent from search. Prior work supports hazard-oriented selection within a stationary population: Zatarain Salazar et al. (2017) stratify a stationary sample by a realized-flow statistic, and Herman et al. (2016) amplify drought severity with a stationary weighted bootstrap.

#### 3.2.2 Stochastic Streamflow Generation and the Candidate Ensemble

Daily multi-site streamflow realizations are generated with the Kirsch-Nowak generator. Cholesky-based monthly generation preserves cross-site and lag correlations (Kirsch et al., 2013), and a nearest-neighbor resampling scheme disaggregates monthly flows to daily flows (Nowak et al., 2010). The generator is fitted to the reconstructed observed record spanning water years 1946 through 2022 (77 water years) and run without climate perturbation. Generation mechanics, the deterministic seed architecture, and the statistical validation of generated flows are documented in Text S2 in Supporting Information S1.

Both matched designs draw from this one generator, and the distinction between them is the mechanism of selection. A design can generate arbitrarily many independent realizations, but it cannot instruct the generator to produce a realization at a prescribed drought severity, because hazard coordinates are emergent properties of a realized sequence and are computable only after the sequence exists. A design that targets hazard coverage must therefore select realizations from a finite set of already-generated sequences rather than generate directly to a target, and the nearest-neighbor assignment step of Section 3.2.4 is a consequence of this fact. A generator purpose-built to emit sequences at prescribed hazard targets is an alternative construction (Zaniolo et al., 2023, 2024), which we do not pursue because it forgoes the simulation-free scalability of selection from a candidate ensemble and requires committing to a small number of discrete, analyst-chosen event types.

The HF design draws on a candidate ensemble $\mathcal{C}$ of $P = 10^6$ realizations sampled independently from the stationary generator. The candidate-ensemble size is set by a nested sizing diagnostic, because the resolution of the severe corners of the hazard space improves with $P$ (Section 3.2.4). Generating candidates involves no system simulation, so the candidate ensemble is inexpensive to enlarge relative to the cost of search. No candidate realization is ever simulated before selection. Only each realization's hazard coordinates and its generation seed are retained, and the $N$ selected members are regenerated exactly on demand from a deterministic, globally indexed random-stream architecture (Text S2 in Supporting Information S1), which is what makes candidate ensembles of order $10^6$ members tractable.

The requirement that the candidate ensemble be sampled independently, rather than by a structured design such as a Latin hypercube, is essential to the validity of the comparison. A uniform random subset of size $N$ taken from an i.i.d. sample has exactly the joint distribution of $N$ fresh independent draws. This distributional equivalence is what makes PS the exact statistical control for HF: the two designs differ only in the rule that selects $N$ members from the same population law, so any difference in re-evaluated robustness is attributable to that rule alone.

#### 3.2.3 Hazard Metric Formulations

Every ensemble scenario is a disjoint, non-overlapping $L$-year realization. Each simulation starts from the fixed initial storage of 80 percent of capacity stated in Section 3.1.2, which constitutes a terminating-simulation design, and the first six months of each realization are excluded from all metrics as the initialization period. A drought event that would straddle a realization boundary is truncated by it, the choice of $L$ bounds this truncation (Section 3.3.1), and event-scale severity is additionally captured at re-evaluation, where records are longer.

Hazard coordinates are computed on the aggregate inflow to the three NYC reservoirs before any system simulation. Let $q_x(t)$ denote the daily aggregate NYC inflow of candidate realization $x$, and let $z_x(\tau)$ denote the corresponding monthly series of the six-month Standardized Streamflow Index (SSI-6), computed with a gamma distribution fitted once per calendar month to the observed record (McKee et al., 1993; Vicente-Serrano et al., 2012). The six-month accumulation period the SSI-6 requires before its first defined value is the basis of the six-month metric exclusion (Section 3.1.2), so hazard coordinates and objectives are defined on the identical window. Eight candidate hazard descriptors are computed for every realization: five drought descriptors derived from run theory applied to the SSI-6 series, and three flood descriptors derived from a peaks-over-threshold analysis of the daily flows.

Drought events are identified from the SSI-6 series with a threshold-based definition with event pooling. An event begins in the first month that $z_x$ falls below zero, is retained as a drought event only if $z_x$ reaches $-1$ during the event, and terminates only after $z_x$ remains non-negative for three consecutive months. Let $E = \{\tau_{\mathrm{on}}, \ldots, \tau_{\mathrm{end}}\}$ denote the months of an event and $\tau_{\mathrm{pk}}$ the month of its minimum index value. The event's deficit volume $D$ and peak depth $S$ are

$$
D(E) \;=\; -\sum_{\tau \in E} \min\big(z_x(\tau),\, 0\big),
\qquad
S(E) \;=\; -\min_{\tau \in E}\, z_x(\tau),
\tag{4}
$$

in units of SSI-months and standard deviations respectively, and the event's duration is $T(E) = |E|$ months. The onset rate $R_{\mathrm{on}}$ and recovery rate $R_{\mathrm{rec}}$ normalize the peak depth by the time taken to reach and to recover from it,

$$
R_{\mathrm{on}}(E) \;=\; \frac{S(E)}{\tau_{\mathrm{pk}} - \tau_{\mathrm{on}} + 1},
\qquad
R_{\mathrm{rec}}(E) \;=\; \frac{S(E)}{\tau_{\mathrm{end}} - \tau_{\mathrm{pk}} + 1}.
\tag{5}
$$

When a realization contains more than one qualifying event, the controlling event $E^{*} = \arg\max_{E} D(E)$, the event with the largest deficit volume and the operationally binding event under fixed initial storage, is scored. All five drought descriptors are zero for a realization with no qualifying event.

The flood descriptors are computed from the daily flows relative to the high-flow threshold $u$, fixed at the 95th percentile of the observed record's daily flow. The critical pulse $P$ of a realization is the exceedance run, the maximal set of consecutive days with $q_x(t) > u$, that contains the realization's maximum daily flow at day $t_{\mathrm{max}}$, so exactly one pulse is scored per realization and no declustering parameter is required. With $\bar{q}$ the mean daily flow of the observed record, the peak magnitude $M$, pulse duration $T_P$, and rise rate $R_{\mathrm{rise}}$ are

$$
M \;=\; \frac{q_x(t_{\mathrm{max}})}{\bar{q}},
\qquad
T_P \;=\; |P|,
\qquad
R_{\mathrm{rise}} \;=\; \frac{1}{\bar{q}} \max_{t \in P,\; t \le t_{\mathrm{max}}} \big( q_x(t) - q_x(t-1) \big).
\tag{6}
$$

The SSI distribution fits, the flood threshold $u$, and the normalizing mean $\bar{q}$ are all fixed once on the observed record and reused for every realization, so hazard coordinates are directly comparable across the candidate ensemble, the realized search ensembles, and the Historical reference. Additional descriptor details are given in Text S3 in Supporting Information S1.

Two scope properties of this hazard space are acknowledged. First, the descriptors are defined on aggregate NYC inflow only. The flows that decide the Montague, Trenton, and flood-gauge objectives include unregulated tributary contributions that the hazard coordinates do not resolve, and a supporting analysis correlates the selected ensemble's hazard coordinates against realized stress at the downstream objective sites (Text S3 in Supporting Information S1). Second, the descriptors characterize a single controlling event per realization, so no frequency, timing, or inter-event descriptor enters the selection, and a realization with two moderate droughts is indistinguishable from a realization with one in this coordinate system. Event multiplicity is left as an explicit extension.

The descriptor set is screened for redundancy on the candidate ensemble before selection, following the index-redundancy logic of Olden and Poff (2003). Degenerate descriptors with near-zero spread are dropped, and near-duplicate pairs, defined by absolute Spearman rank correlation of at least 0.95, are pruned to a single canonical member so that one hazard concept cannot enter the selection distance twice under two names. On this population the screen retains all eight candidate descriptors. The largest pairwise rank correlation, 0.88 between deficit volume and duration, falls below the pruning threshold, is stable across candidate-ensemble sizes from $2 \times 10^3$ to $10^6$ members, and the retained set is insensitive to tightening the threshold to 0.90. Six of the retained descriptors serve as the selection axes: deficit volume, peak depth, onset rate, and recovery rate on the drought side, and peak magnitude and pulse duration on the flood side. The two remaining descriptors are excluded from the selection distance, drought duration as the most redundant member of the retained set (rank correlation 0.87 with deficit volume, with a quasi-discrete, months-valued tail) and flood rise rate as the flood descriptor most correlated with the retained flood pair (rank correlation 0.60 with peak magnitude). Limiting the dimension of the selection space matters because the selection's reach into the severe corners of the hazard space weakens as axes are added at fixed candidate-ensemble size (Section 3.2.4). The full redundancy structure of the descriptor set, together with a selection-invariance analysis quantifying the weighting that correlated axes induce in the selection distance, is reported in Text S3 in Supporting Information S1.

#### 3.2.4 Space-Filling Scenario Selection in Hazard Space

The hazard-filling construction selects the $N$ members of the search ensemble so that they cover the six-dimensional selection space of Section 3.2.3 in absolute, range-scaled magnitude units. Each selection axis $a$ is scaled to the unit interval by its robust range across the candidate ensemble, using the per-axis 1st and 99th percentiles $h_a^{(1)}$ and $h_a^{(99)}$,

$$
\tilde{h}_a(x) \;=\; \min\!\Big(1, \; \max\!\Big(0, \; \frac{h_a(x) - h_a^{(1)}}{h_a^{(99)} - h_a^{(1)}} \Big)\Big),
\tag{7}
$$

with members beyond the percentile bounds clipped to the faces of the unit hypercube, so that no single axis dominates distances while the spacing within each axis remains proportional to physical magnitude. Robust percentile bounds, rather than the sample extremes, make the selection geometry a stable functional of the underlying population. The sample minimum and maximum of a right-skewed hazard metric are non-convergent extreme order statistics, so a full-range scaling would grow with the candidate-ensemble size $P$, vary materially across independent candidate-ensemble constructions, and allow a single outlier to compress the bulk of the population into a corner of the hypercube. The clipped mass is bounded at one percent per axis tail, and a sensitivity sweep over the bound settings confirms the choice empirically. Full-range scaling degrades realized coverage roughly threefold through fixation on outliers, while coverage and tail representation vary smoothly across nearby robust-percentile settings (Text S4 in Supporting Information S1).

The selection then proceeds in two steps. First, $N$ anchor points $\mathbf{u}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{u}_N$ are drawn as a Latin hypercube sample in the scaled hazard space $[0,1]^m$ with $m = 6$. Second, each anchor is assigned to its nearest unselected candidate realization in Euclidean distance,

$$
x_{(i)} \;=\; \underset{x \,\in\, \mathcal{C} \setminus \{x_{(1)}, \ldots, x_{(i-1)}\}}{\arg\min} \; \big\lVert \tilde{\mathbf{h}}(x) - \mathbf{u}_i \big\rVert_2,
\qquad i = 1, \ldots, N,
\tag{8}
$$

and the selected set $\{x_{(1)}, \ldots, x_{(N)}\}$ is the HF search ensemble. The procedure is deterministic given its anchor seed, involves no iterative optimization of a coverage criterion, and has no tuning parameters. Because Latin hypercube sampling stratifies every axis marginally regardless of dimension, the procedure's guarantee is stratified marginal coverage with tail representation on each selection axis. Joint coverage is reported descriptively against a random subset of the same size and dimension rather than claimed as uniform (Text S4 in Supporting Information S1). An optimal one-to-one assignment of anchors to candidates is indistinguishable from the sequential nearest-neighbor rule of Equation 8 on every coverage metric, so the simpler rule is retained (Text S4 in Supporting Information S1).

The strength of the tail representation that the procedure achieves is set jointly by the candidate-ensemble size and the dimension of the selection space. The expected distance from an anchor to its nearest candidate scales as $P^{-1/m}$ on $m$ axes, so at fixed $P$ each added axis increases the anchor-to-candidate distances and pulls selected members back toward the dense center of the population. In the supporting diagnostics, the mean assignment distance roughly triples between four-axis and eight-axis selection on the same candidate ensemble, with per-axis tail representation falling accordingly (Text S4 in Supporting Information S1). The design therefore restricts the selection space to the six axes of Section 3.2.3 and sets $P = 10^6$ from a nested sizing diagnostic, in which the selection is repeated on nested prefixes of a single large candidate ensemble, each an exact i.i.d. sample of its intermediate size under the deterministic seed architecture (Text S2 in Supporting Information S1). At the campaign configuration, every selection axis places at least 31 percent of selected members above the candidate ensemble's 90th percentile, roughly three times the 10 percent share that i.i.d. sampling delivers, and this realized enrichment is re-measured on every campaign construction as a build diagnostic (Text S4 in Supporting Information S1).

Scaling the selection axes by absolute magnitude, rather than by probability, is the choice that makes the construction a genuine intervention on the search rather than a variance-reduction device. The hazard marginals of a stationary stochastic generator are strongly right-skewed, so most candidate realizations cluster near the center of the hazard space and few occupy the severe corners. Filling the range of each axis uniformly therefore draws selected members from the sparse corners far more often than their frequency in the population, and the severe drought and flood conditions that decide reservoir performance are over-represented in the search ensemble relative to their probability under the generator. The rationale for uniform coverage of a stress space, as opposed to probability-faithful sampling of it, descends from the decision-scaling and scenario-neutral traditions, in which climate stress spaces are sampled uniformly to map system response rather than to estimate expectations (Brown et al., 2012; Culley et al., 2016; Fowler et al., 2024), and from the synthetic drought generation of Herman et al. (2016), which likewise over-weights severe conditions during vulnerability assessment.

An alternative construction that transforms each axis by its empirical cumulative distribution before filling would instead reproduce the population's marginal frequencies and alter only the joint dependence among axes. That rank-space variant is registered as a non-campaign sensitivity analysis because it isolates how much of any design effect is attributable specifically to tail over-representation.

The two matched designs consequently present different scenario distributions to the search. PS presents the generator's own distribution, and HF presents a distribution deliberately shifted toward severe hazard conditions, so any objective aggregated over the HF search ensemble is computed under a measure that over-weights the conditions in which policies are hardest to satisfy. The shift changes which policies the optimizer retains, not merely a reported number, which is why it is a design intervention worth testing and why search objectives are never compared across designs. Nor could the shift be reproduced by hardening the across-ensemble filter $\Psi$ on a probability-faithful ensemble, because no aggregation operator can recover information about severe sequences that the ensemble does not contain. The intervention operates on the information presented to the search rather than on the risk attitude taken toward it. Whether over-representing severe hazard conditions during search yields policies that are more robust under deeply uncertain re-evaluation, or merely tailors policies to conditions that re-evaluation does not reward, is precisely the empirical content of the first research question, and the common re-evaluation ensemble of Section 3.4 is what makes the two outcomes distinguishable.

### 3.3 Computational Experiment

#### 3.3.1 Search Ensemble Sizing

Both matched designs use $N = 100$ realizations of $L = 10$ years, amounting to 1,000 scenario-years per function evaluation, and both run to an equal number of function evaluations (NFE). Because $N$ and $L$ are common, the per-evaluation simulation cost is identical across designs, so equal NFE and equal simulated scenario-years coincide and ensemble composition is not confounded with search effort.

The common $N$ and $L$ are required rather than convenient. If $L$ differed across designs, the selection rule would be confounded with record length, and at a fixed per-evaluation cost $N$ and $L$ trade off directly. The value of $N$ is bounded below by the coverage requirement of the hazard-filling construction. The Latin hypercube anchors stratify each selection axis into $N$ bins, so marginal coverage sharpens with $N$, while enlarging $N$ at fixed candidate-ensemble size lowers per-axis tail representation because the candidate ensemble contains only $P/10$ members above the 90th percentile of any axis and additional selections increasingly draw from the dense center (Text S4 in Supporting Information S1). A long-record alternative, for example $L = 50$ years forcing $N \approx 20$ at fixed cost, would leave too few anchors for meaningful stratification. The value $L = 10$ years in turn exceeds the duration of the DRB drought of record in the 1960s, which lasted four to five years, plus its onset and recovery, so a design-basis event fits within a single realization and duration-type hazard descriptors are not truncation-limited. The per-evaluation ensemble of 100 realizations is smaller than the 1,000-realization convention of the regional water-supply planning literature. The basis for it is the annual-unit aggregation of Equation 3, which yields 900 metric-bearing annual units per evaluation, comparable to the sample on which that literature stabilizes the same tail operators (Section 3.1.2). The sampling variability of the worst-first-percentile operator at this ensemble size is quantified by the epsilon-calibration bootstrap (Text S5 in Supporting Information S1). The HIST design, with one realization of 77 years, cannot be matched in these dimensions and is reported as a reference throughout.

Each search runs to 500,000 NFE, within the range used for comparable reservoir policy-search problems (Quinn et al., 2017; Bartholomew and Kwakkel, 2020). Because the runtime archive (Section 3.3.3) records the approximation set at intermediate NFE levels, the adequacy of this choice is assessed against observed convergence behavior, and the design comparison is recomputable at earlier NFE levels at re-evaluation cost only, which tests whether the design ranking depends on the search length.

#### 3.3.2 Replication of Ensemble Construction and Search

The experiment separates two sources of variability by design. An ensemble draw is one complete construction of a scenario design carried out with a fresh seed. For PS, a draw is a fresh i.i.d. sample of $N$ realizations. For HF, a draw is a fresh candidate ensemble together with a fresh anchor sample. Reconstructing the candidate ensemble within each draw is essential, because generating the candidates is part of the hazard-filling construction, and holding the candidate ensemble fixed across draws would understate HF's construction variability relative to PS as an artifact of the replication scheme. A seed is an independent trial of the search algorithm on a fixed draw, run to account for the stochastic initialization and operator dynamics of evolutionary search. Each matched design runs $K = 3$ draws with $S = 2$ seeds per draw, and the HIST design, which has zero construction variability, runs $K = 1$ draw across the same number of seeds.

Following standard practice in the MOEA literature, the approximation sets from a draw's seeds are merged and non-dominated sorted into a single per-draw Pareto-approximate set (Hadka and Reed, 2015; Zatarain Salazar et al., 2017), so seed-to-seed search variability is absorbed within each draw rather than analyzed as an experimental factor. Search reliability is not a subject of this study, and the reliability of MM Borg's epsilon-dominance archiving and randomized restarts across random seed trials is documented (Hadka and Reed, 2013, 2015). Seed-level convergence is monitored through the runtime diagnostics of Text S7 in Supporting Information S1. The unit of analysis for the between-design comparison is therefore the draw, and draw-level results are reported transparently so that the design effect is read against the observed construction variability. This replication structure distinguishes the present comparison from its nearest antecedent, Eker and Kwakkel (2018), which compared a single designed scenario set against a single random set and could not separate a genuine design effect from the variability of a single construction.

#### 3.3.3 Many-Objective Search with the Multi-Master Borg MOEA

All searches use the multi-master Borg multiobjective evolutionary algorithm (MM Borg; Hadka and Reed, 2013, 2015), whose epsilon-dominance archiving, adaptive operator selection, and randomized restarts have proven reliable across many-objective water resources applications (Reed et al., 2013; Zatarain Salazar et al., 2016, 2017). Each combination of design, draw, and seed is an independent search on the Purdue Anvil system, run as four islands of 254 evaluator ranks (1,021 MPI ranks on eight nodes) to the 500,000-NFE limit, with the calibrated epsilon-dominance precisions of Table 2 shared by every search. Runtime archive snapshots are recorded at a fixed NFE interval. The parallelization measurements that size this configuration are reported in Text S8 in Supporting Information S1.

Within each design, search progress is monitored through runtime epsilon-progress and hypervolume diagnostics, computed against reference sets constructed separately for each design and draw, because different draws carry different search ensembles and their objective values are likewise not commensurable, and reported per seed (following the runtime-metrics conventions of Reed et al., 2013, and Hadka and Reed, 2015, in Text S7 in Supporting Information S1; these diagnostics are being finalized). These quantities are read purely as convergence diagnostics and are never compared across designs, because objective values computed on different search ensembles are not commensurable, and a reference set merged across ensemble constructions scores each design against a frontier it helped define (Zatarain Salazar et al., 2017). Convergence assessment is not used as a stopping rule, so no design benefits from a tuned termination criterion, and both matched designs execute the identical NFE limit.

### 3.4 Deep Uncertainty Re-Evaluation and Robustness Analysis

#### 3.4.1 The Deeply Uncertain Re-Evaluation Ensemble

Every design's final Pareto-approximate set is stress tested on a single common re-evaluation ensemble, following the deep uncertainty (DU) re-evaluation approach of Trindade et al. (2017), using the trimmed model configuration of Section 2.2 with the policy-independent boundary releases simulated once per realization. All cross-design comparisons are made there. The re-evaluation ensemble is never used during any search and is never the source of any search ensemble. It is generated from a reserved seed domain disjoint from that of the candidate ensemble, and any collision between the search-side and test-side seed domains is treated as a hard error, which guards against the failure mode of testing on scenarios the search has seen (Bonham et al., 2024).

The re-evaluation ensemble is constructed to span a deeply uncertain climate-forcing envelope rather than to represent a probability distribution. The forcing is a monthly change factor applied to the generator's fitted moments, parameterized as a low-order harmonic in the log change factor whose amplitudes govern annual volume, seasonal amplitude, and the snowmelt-shoulder shape, with amplitude ranges taken from a 54-member CMIP6 multimodel ensemble of monthly streamflow change profiles (Text S6 in Supporting Information S1). The adjustment preserves each month's coefficient of variation, so the forcing perturbs the mean seasonal profile without independently altering relative variability (an independent variance axis was evaluated and excluded on hazard-coverage grounds, Text S6 in Supporting Information S1). Each state-of-the-world (SOW) is one Latin hypercube point in the three-dimensional amplitude space, sampled over the full empirical range of the CMIP6-fitted amplitudes widened by a 25 percent margin, an envelope deliberately wider than any variation the search ensembles contain. Each SOW is crossed with stochastic realizations that sample natural variability within that state.

The ensemble comprises 1,000 SOWs with 25 realizations per SOW and 50-year records, for 25,000 realizations and 1.25 million scenario-years. The SOW count fixes the Monte Carlo resolution of any reported satisficing fraction at plus or minus 1.6 percentage points in the worst case, the standard error of a binomial fraction estimated over 1,000 SOWs, which is maximal at a fraction of one half. Each SOW's 1,225 metric-bearing annual units match the sample at which the tail operators of Section 3.1.2 stabilize, so the within-SOW performance estimate is resolved at least as well as one search evaluation. The 50-year records, five times the search-record length, additionally test sustained operation, because consecutive droughts are experienced on carried-over storage and the running-average delivery accounting matures, neither of which a terminating 10-year realization can express. Where the re-evaluation ensemble itself is placed in the hazard space, its hazard coordinates are computed on disjoint, October-aligned 10-year sub-windows, so they are commensurable with the candidate ensemble's window convention. The adequacy of both sample sizes is verified after re-evaluation by SOW-subsample and realization-subsample stability of the design rankings, computed from the stored re-evaluation output at no additional simulation cost. This construction, a Latin hypercube sample of SOWs crossed with stochastic realizations, is the standard deep uncertainty re-evaluation design of the robustness literature (Kasprzyk et al., 2013; Trindade et al., 2017; Bartholomew and Kwakkel, 2020; Quinn et al., 2020; Gold et al., 2022).

Two properties of the re-evaluation ensemble govern the interpretation of the comparison. First, the search ensembles are drawn from the unperturbed stationary generator, whereas the re-evaluation ensemble spans a forced climate envelope that no search ensemble contains. The re-evaluation therefore measures whether hazard-space coverage of the generator's natural variability produces policies that generalize to conditions absent from search, a stronger test than re-evaluating on the search distribution, and one that keeps the re-evaluation ensemble structurally distinct from both matched designs so that it favors neither. Second, no robustness value reported in this study is an expectation, because under deep uncertainty there is no probability measure over the forcing space (Lamontagne et al., 2018). A satisficing fraction computed over the re-evaluation ensemble is a coverage-weighted count over a designed exploration of the forcing envelope, and it is reported as such. The cross-design comparison is commensurable because the re-evaluation ensemble is identical across designs, not because it is probability-faithful.

Following Quinn et al. (2020), we do not claim that the re-evaluation ensemble is scenario-neutral, because no experimental design is, and all design rankings are conditional on this one reference ensemble. Composition-sensitivity analyses, in which hazard-restricted and envelope-restricted subsets of the re-evaluation ensemble are re-scored to probe whether the ranking depends on the region of the forcing space emphasized, are reported in Text S10 in Supporting Information S1. The construction inherits the historical record's interannual wet-dry persistence, so persistence is stressed in neither the search population nor the re-evaluation ensemble, and all claims are scoped to hold under historical-level interannual persistence (Text S2 in Supporting Information S1). NYC and NJ demands are likewise held constant at the Decree maxima, the institutionally binding upper bounds on deliveries under the governance structure of Section 2.1, so no demand-growth axis enters either search or re-evaluation.

#### 3.4.2 Robustness and Regret: Comparison Metrics

The primary robustness measure for the first research question is satisficing robustness under Starr's domain criterion (Starr, 1962). Following Herman et al. (2014), we define robustness as the fraction of sampled re-evaluation scenarios in which a policy satisfies all performance criteria, the standard satisficing measure of the regional water-supply planning literature (Herman et al., 2015; Trindade et al., 2017; Gold et al., 2022, 2023) and the fastest-converging robustness family in ensemble-size testing, with accurate rankings from 50 to 300 scenarios (Bonham et al., 2024). For a single policy $\mathbf{x}$, the satisficing fraction over the re-evaluation ensemble is

$$
S(\mathbf{x}) \;=\; \frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{re}}} \sum_{j=1}^{N_{\mathrm{re}}} \Lambda_j(\mathbf{x}),
\qquad
\Lambda_j(\mathbf{x}) \;=\; \prod_{i} \mathbf{1}\!\left[\, f_{i}(\mathbf{x}, s_j) \;\text{meets}\; c_i \,\right],
\tag{9}
$$

where $N_{\mathrm{re}}$ is the number of re-evaluation realizations, $f_i(\mathbf{x}, s_j)$ is policy $\mathbf{x}$'s whole-record temporal metric (Table 2) for objective $i$ on realization $s_j$, and $c_i$ is that objective's satisficing criterion. Because realizations within a SOW share a forcing point and are not independent, the uncertainty of ensemble-level robustness values is assessed at the SOW level. [Open item: the primary robustness unit, the re-evaluation realization of Equation 9 or a SOW-level aggregation, will be fixed to the prevailing convention of the MOEA robustness literature before any re-evaluation result is inspected.]

Each draw's merged search yields a Pareto-approximate set rather than one policy, so a draw-level scalar is required before the between-design comparison of Section 3.3.2 can operate. The pre-specified draw-level measure is the maximum satisficing fraction attained by any policy in the draw's re-evaluated set, which answers the practical question of whether a design can find a robust policy at all. Because the maximum over a set of policies tends to increase with the number of policies in the set, three companions accompany it: the per-objective satisficing decomposition, the set cardinalities reported by design and draw, and the fraction of each draw's re-evaluated set exceeding a given satisficing level, traced across the criterion sweep, a set-level measure that is less sensitive to cardinality. No single scalar carries the entire comparison. The criteria $c_i$ are anchored on Decree and FFMP quantities wherever one exists and are otherwise conventions descended from stakeholder elicitation practice (Zeff et al., 2014). The unanchored criteria are therefore swept over a common stringency axis, and the design comparison is reported as a difference that holds, or fails to hold, across the range of defensible criteria, following the demonstration of Quinn et al. (2020) that robustness rankings degrade as satisficing criteria become more stringent (sweep construction and threshold-margin diagnostics in Text S10 in Supporting Information S1). [Open item: the criterion anchor values.]

Four secondary measures are reported alongside the primary one. Univariate satisficing gives the per-objective decomposition of Equation 9. The mean across re-evaluation realizations is the risk-neutral reference, and the worst realization is the risk-averse reference, both reported because the choice of robustness metric is known to change robustness rankings (Herman et al., 2015; Giuliani and Castelletti, 2016; McPhail et al., 2018). The signed improvement over current operations is the orientation-signed mean difference between each policy and the default 2017 FFMP policy simulated on the same re-evaluation ensemble, and is the risk-neutral summary of the regret family defined next. Agreement among the rankings of the merged re-evaluated policies induced by these measures is summarized with Kendall's rank correlation $\tau_b$ (McPhail et al., 2020), and because a robustness scalar can be stable, optimizable, and still misleading (Huang et al., 2025), the raw performance distributions on the re-evaluation ensemble are co-reported alongside every robustness value.

##### Incumbent-relative regret

The second research question asks whether re-optimized policies improve some outcomes without degrading others below current performance, which is a statement about the downside relative to the status quo rather than about an average. A mean signed improvement cannot express it, because a policy may hold a comfortably positive mean while performing worse than current operations in a substantial minority of futures. We therefore report a regret family whose reference is the incumbent 2017 FFMP policy evaluated in the *same* state of the world. Writing $\sigma_i = +1$ for a maximized objective and $-1$ for a minimized one, and $\bar{f}_i(\cdot, \theta)$ for the within-SOW mean of the whole-record metric, the signed advantage of policy $\mathbf{x}$ over the incumbent $\mathbf{b}$ is

$$
D_i(\mathbf{x}, \theta) \;=\; \sigma_i\left[\bar{f}_i(\mathbf{x}, \theta) - \bar{f}_i(\mathbf{b}, \theta)\right],
\tag{10}
$$

oriented so that positive always means better than current operations. Regret is its adverse part, $G_i = \max(0, -D_i)$, reported per objective as the mean and the 90th percentile across states of the world, and accompanied by the mean gain $\max(0, D_i)$, because a policy can attain zero regret merely by reproducing the incumbent.

Three properties of this construction are deliberate. First, the reference is a fixed decision alternative evaluated per scenario, which McPhail et al. (2018) admit as a performance-value transformation — alternatives may be scored "relative to that of other decision alternatives or a benchmark", using "some type of baseline performance for a given scenario" — while naming and testing only the best-alternative and own-median variants. It is equivalently Savage (1951) regret on the action set the Decree Parties actually face, namely retaining the Flexible Flow Management Program or adopting a candidate revision, since renegotiation under unanimity is a two-alternative decision rather than a free choice over an archive. Because the incumbent is external and fixed, the measure does not move when a design is added to or dropped from the comparison. Second, restricting to the adverse subset is the scenario-subset selection of the undesirable-deviations metric (Kwakkel et al., 2016), with the reference changed from a policy's own median to the incumbent, and the per-objective deviation followed by a 90th percentile across states of the world is the form of Herman et al. (2015), adopted with their justification that a high percentile reflects the tail of poor performance while limiting sensitivity to outliers. We do not report maximum regret, which does not converge at attainable ensemble sizes for tail-defined objectives (Bonham et al., 2024). Third, regret is reported in each objective's own natural units and is never aggregated across objectives. Ratio normalization is unusable here because the incumbent's flood exceedance and both deficit metrics are exactly zero in a large share of re-evaluation realizations, which would render those states of the world undefined and drop precisely the benign ones; Herman et al. (2015) encountered the same degeneracy and chose their denominator to avoid it. The two published alternatives are also unavailable: normalizing on the achievable span to a perfect-foresight optimum (Cohen et al., 2021) requires one optimization per scenario, and rescaling over the evaluated alternative set (Sunkara et al., 2023) would make the scale depend on which designs are compared.

Cross-objective and cross-design summaries are therefore carried by frequencies, which require no scale. Let $\tau_i$ be a per-objective tolerance. The no-harm frequency

$$
\Pi_\tau(\mathbf{x}) \;=\; \frac{1}{N_\theta}\sum_{\theta} \prod_i \mathbf{1}\!\left[\, D_i(\mathbf{x}, \theta) \ge -\tau_i \,\right]
\tag{11}
$$

is the fraction of states of the world in which no objective is degraded beyond tolerance, and $\Pi_0$ is a weak Pareto improvement on the incumbent. Cohen et al. (2021) impose exactly this condition as a filter, retaining only solutions that outperform the status quo in every re-evaluation; reporting it as a graded frequency retains the policies whose trade-off against the incumbent is the substance of the question. We also report the per-objective harm frequencies, the mean number of objectives simultaneously degraded, and a Decree-Party decomposition in which a party is counted as worse off when *any* of its objectives is degraded. The party form is a disjunction rather than a sum or an average because a loss to one party is not compensable by a gain to another under unanimity; Sunkara et al. (2023) show that compensating aggregations across actors can make a robustness measure appear stable only because failures in one sector are offset by successes in another. The tolerance is set at $\tau_i = k\,u_i$, so that the sweep over $k$ reads as the number of just-noticeable differences of degradation tolerated, and the answer to the second research question is reported as the tolerance at which it holds rather than at a single arbitrary value. The unit $u_i = \max(\epsilon_i, \tau_i^{\mathrm{floor}})$ combines the signal-scale resolution of objective $i$ with the noise floor of its state-of-the-world mean, estimated from the incumbent policy's own re-evaluation. Both terms are needed because a single $k$ is shared across all eight objectives: an objective whose resolution lies below its estimator noise would have every rung of the ladder firing on Monte Carlo noise while the same rung sat far outside the noise on every other objective, so one mis-scaled axis would silently set what a rung means everywhere.

Because $k$ and the margin $\delta$ are the only quantities in this comparison that the data do not determine, both are fixed in advance by rules rather than by inspection, and the admissible anchors are restricted by what they could bias. Anchors drawn from estimator noise or measurement resolution are admissible for both, since they establish a floor rather than a preference and are computable from the incumbent alone before any policy exists. Anchors drawn from the distribution of candidate-policy regret are inadmissible for either, because that distribution is the quantity under test. The margin is anchored on within-design variation only: the spread of $\Pi_\tau$ across independent draws of the same design carries no design effect by construction, and it is the variability a between-design difference must exceed given that the draw is the unit of analysis. Because a non-inferiority claim is flattered by an insensitive measurement, the headline tolerance is the smallest rung clearing the noise floor rather than the largest defensible one, and we report the tolerance range over which the comparison is neither starved nor saturated. We further verify assay sensitivity: if the comparison cannot separate the unmatched Historical reference, which is expected to be worse, from the matched designs, then a null between the matched designs is evidence of an insensitive measurement rather than of equivalent designs, and is reported as such. Full rules, diagnostics, and the pre-registration order of operations are given in Text S12 in Supporting Information S1.

The regret family is complementary to the satisficing measure rather than redundant with it. The satisficing criteria are fixed thresholds anchored, where no external goalpost exists, on the performance the observed system already attains, whereas the regret reference is the incumbent's performance in each state of the world and therefore degrades as the forcing does. Where a fixed criterion is unattainable for every policy in a state of the world, the domain criterion is zero for all of them and cannot discriminate, which is the saturation failure mode Bonham et al. (2024) identify; the regret measure continues to separate policies there. For the same reason, robustness and regret are read off the *same* policy whenever they are compared, and the joint result is presented in the plane they span together with each design's non-dominated frontier within it.

Two regret references are deliberately excluded. Best-in-set regret defines its reference by the best-performing policy in each scenario, so removing one design from the comparison changes every other design's score, and it is the slowest-converging metric family in the ensemble-size testing of Bonham et al. (2024); Herman et al. (2015) additionally found their two regret measures to agree closely, so little is lost. Baseline regret in the sense of Cohen et al. (2021) requires one perfect-foresight optimization per scenario and does not scale to candidate ensembles of order $10^6$ members, so we cite it as motivation and do not compute it. We note also that the percent-deviation measure of Kasprzyk et al. (2013), and its generalization as the first regret variant of Herman et al. (2015), reference a policy's own performance in a baseline *state of the world* rather than the performance of a baseline *policy*; it measures sensitivity to assumptions about the future and is a different quantity from the one defined above. We additionally identify the re-evaluation realizations in which no policy from either design meets the satisficing criteria. This attainability analysis separates poor search performance from scenarios that no evaluated policy can satisfy, a distinction Shavazipour et al. (2021) found to affect 23 percent of their test scenarios, and each design's satisficing fraction is read against the attainable ceiling it implies.

Taken together the two families test a hypothesis the literature gives reason to doubt. Bartholomew and Kwakkel (2020) find both that the more robustness is considered during search the better it survives re-evaluation, and that robustness is generally purchased at the expense of attainable performance in individual scenarios, the price of robustness in the sense of Bertsimas and Sim (2004); Giuliani and Castelletti (2016) show that policies designed under one across-scenario aggregation and scored under another are systematically dominated. Hazard Filling searches under a deliberately severity-shifted measure and is scored under the re-evaluation measure, so it is precisely the case those results describe. We pre-register the expectation that it attains higher satisficing robustness without a higher incumbent-relative regret, evaluated at a declared tolerance and a declared non-inferiority margin, with the draw as the unit of analysis. Huang et al. (2025) give the specific form a failure would take, namely that over-weighting extreme conditions can skew multi-objective trade-offs so that particular objectives dominate the selection; the per-objective regret vector is the instrument that would detect it, and such a result is reportable rather than a failed test. Whether any price is instead paid in the benign futures is examined by decomposing both measures over terciles of the dominant forcing factor.

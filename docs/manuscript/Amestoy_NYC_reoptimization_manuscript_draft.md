## 1. Introduction

- **P1. The Delaware River Basin stakes.** Open with the decision problem, not the method, so that the re-optimization question is motivated before the methodological one. The three NYC-operated headwater reservoirs supply roughly half of the city's drinking water while sustaining mandated flows that protect downstream states and hold the salt front below Philadelphia's water supply intakes. The operating rules balancing these obligations, most recently the 2017 FFMP, are negotiated under a unanimity requirement among the five Decree parties and revisited on multi-year renegotiation cycles. Those rules were tuned during a comparatively wet epoch: the 1960s drought of record predates the basin's persistent post-1970 pluvial, and paleoclimate reconstructions indicate the basin can produce droughts at least as severe (Pederson et al., 2013; Devineni et al., 2013 — to be imported to the collection). Whether renegotiation-scale adjustments of the FFMP's negotiated constants could improve outcomes for multiple parties simultaneously is an institutionally live question, and it motivates the second research question directly.

- **P2. Many-objective search and the evaluation ensemble.** Simulation-based many-objective evolutionary search is the established framework for exploring operating-rule adaptation and revealing trade-offs among competing water uses (Reed et al., 2013; Giuliani et al., 2016; Herman et al., 2020). Introduce the scenario-design challenge with care: the search ensemble is fixed before search and reused across every one of the hundreds of thousands of candidate-policy evaluations, so its composition is the lens through which the algorithm sees every policy it considers, and the hydrologic conditions it contains determine which stresses shape the retained policies. In most applications the search ensemble is inherited rather than designed, with two prevailing constructions, the historical record and an independent sample from a fitted stochastic generator, and neither is chosen for the stress content it presents to the search.

- **P3. The evidence that composition matters is real and two-sided.** Supporting evidence: the properties of training scenarios determine out-of-sample robustness, with high-regret subsets matching full-ensemble performance at roughly one third of the cost (Cohen et al., 2021), the drought type embedded in a training ensemble changes the infrastructure installed and the state variables a policy consults (Zaniolo et al., 2023), and policies calibrated to a single sequence overfit its event ordering (Brodeur et al., 2020). Cautionary evidence: scenarios selected for policy relevance and diversity did not outperform an arbitrary set (Eker and Kwakkel, 2018), scenario selection moves robustness values far more than robustness rankings (McPhail et al., 2020), and policies designed under one across-scenario aggregation and scored under another are systematically dominated by policies designed under the scoring measure (Giuliani and Castelletti, 2016), a direct caution for any method that alters the scenario distribution during search. Close with the design consequence: the question is open, and answering it requires an experiment built to detect the effect if it exists and to report a bounded, informative null if it does not.

- **P4. Three spaces.** Distinguish the input space (generator parameters, climate multipliers, and demand factors, where a sampled point is one of the states-of-the-world, SOWs, of the robust-decision-making lineage: Kasprzyk et al., 2013; Trindade et al., 2017), the outcome space (simulation outputs, the objective values of a policy under a scenario), and, between them, the hazard space, the space of hydrologic hazard metrics computed directly on each realized streamflow sequence before any system simulation. Hazard coordinates are properties of the scenario rather than of the simulated response, and they are computable for millions of candidate sequences at negligible cost. One sentence on the nearest existing construct, the exposure space of the scenario-neutral tradition (Culley et al., 2016; Fowler et al., 2024): exposure spaces are attribute targets imposed on generator inputs, whereas hazard coordinates are measured on realized sequences. Keep this paragraph lean and defer the full taxonomy to Section 3.1.3.

- **P5. The gap, organized by the dependency each prior approach carries.** A probability-faithful generator sample populates the common center of the hazard distribution densely and the severe corners sparsely, although the severe corners are where reservoir policies are decided. Prior constructions that control hazard content either preserve the generating probability (one-dimensional stratification of a stationary population: Zatarain Salazar et al., 2017), commit to a few discrete analyst-chosen event types at generation time (Herman et al., 2016; Zaniolo et al., 2023, 2024), or require simulating or optimizing every candidate scenario through the system model (Hilbers et al., 2019; Giudici et al., 2020; and the baseline-regret selection of Cohen et al., 2021, which requires one perfect-foresight optimization per candidate). Space-filling subsampling of SOW ensembles exists for post-hoc robustness ranking rather than for search (Bonham et al., 2024), small carried ensembles materially change the solutions found (Watson and Kasprzyk, 2017), and vulnerability-plus-diversity selection has been applied outside the search loop (Carlsen et al., 2016). Close with the open problem as Bartholomew and Kwakkel (2020) state it: "Research is needed into the selection [of] scenarios which as a set contain the appropriately stressing conditions against which solutions have to be robust, while also capturing the scenarios under which one would like to have near optimal performance."

**P6. Contribution statement (drafted).** This study contributes hazard filling, a search-ensemble construction that selects scenarios from a large candidate pool of synthetic streamflow sequences so that the retained members cover the hydrologic hazard space, spanning the full range of drought and flood conditions the generating process can produce, including severe conditions that independent sampling visits only rarely. Because hazard coordinates are computed on the streamflow sequences alone, the selection is simulation-free, scales to candidate pools of order one million sequences, and transfers across objective formulations. The selection procedure is deliberately the simplest defensible space-filling design, placing Latin hypercube anchor points in the hazard space and assigning each anchor to its nearest unselected candidate sequence: it is deterministic given its anchors and has no tuning parameters. We test this construction against the prevailing default, an independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) sample from the same stochastic generator, on the re-optimization of New York City's (NYC) Delaware River Basin reservoir operations within the Flexible Flow Management Program (FFMP) rule structure, simulated with the open-source Pywr-DRB model (Hamilton et al., 2024). To our knowledge, this is the first controlled comparison of hazard-space scenario selection against probabilistic sampling for many-objective policy search on a real water system. The comparison holds ensemble size, record length, and the function-evaluation budget fixed, and evaluates both designs' policies on one common deeply uncertain re-evaluation ensemble (Section 3).

**P7. Research questions (drafted).** We use this experimental design to address two research questions. First, does constructing the search ensemble by space-filling coverage of the hazard space, rather than by independent sampling from the same stochastic generator, change the robustness of the resulting Pareto-approximate policies under deeply uncertain re-evaluation? Second, do re-optimized FFMP policies exist that simultaneously improve multiple stakeholder outcomes, spanning water supply reliability, Montague and Trenton flow-target compliance, downstream flood exposure, and minimum reservoir storage, without degrading others below current performance on the same re-evaluation ensemble?

**P8. Roadmap (drafted).** The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 describes the Delaware River Basin test case, the FFMP operating rules, and the Pywr-DRB simulation model. Section 3 details the methodology, comprising the three scenario designs and the hazard-filling construction, the many-objective problem formulation, the search configuration, and the deep uncertainty re-evaluation and robustness analysis. Section 4 presents the results, Section 5 discusses their implications, and Section 6 concludes.

## 2. The Delaware River Basin Test Case

### 2.1 Institutional Water Management

The Delaware River Basin (DRB) spans New York, Pennsylvania, New Jersey, and Delaware and supplies approximately one half of NYC's drinking water through three upper-basin reservoirs (Cannonsville, Pepacton, and Neversink; combined capacity approximately 271 billion gallons) whose diversions pass through the Rondout balancing reservoir and the Delaware Aqueduct. Basin operations are governed by the 1954 U.S. Supreme Court Decree in *New Jersey v. New York*, which sets the NYC diversion allocation at 800 million gallons per day (MGD), entitles New Jersey (NJ) to a 100 MGD diversion, and obligates NYC to make compensating reservoir releases that maintain a minimum flow of 1,750 cubic feet per second (cfs, equivalent to 1,131 MGD) on the Delaware River mainstem at Montague, NJ. The Office of the Delaware River Master within the U.S. Geological Survey administers these Decree provisions. A separate flow objective of 3,000 cfs (1,939 MGD) at Trenton, NJ, established under the Delaware River Basin Commission's Water Code and implemented through the basin's operating agreements, protects downstream ecosystems and limits upstream migration of the salt front toward the Philadelphia and Camden water supply intakes. Changes to the operation of NYC's Delaware reservoirs require unanimous consent of the five Decree parties.

The system's operational tension was established empirically by the 1960s drought of record (1964-1967), during which storage could not simultaneously support the decreed NYC diversion and the Montague compensating releases, emergency negotiations among the Decree parties coupled diversion curtailments with flow-target reductions, and the salt front advanced to within approximately 13 km of Philadelphia's drinking water intakes (Hogarty, 1970; Kolesar and Serio, 2011). Severe drought forces NYC's authorized diversion, the Montague compensating releases, and downstream ecological flows into direct competition for the same depleted storage, while wet-period storage management trades flood mitigation against drought reserve.

### 2.2 The 2017 FFMP Operating Rules

Since 2007, NYC reservoir releases and diversions have been managed under successive versions of the Flexible Flow Management Program, most recently the 2017 FFMP (Decree Parties, 2017), whose storage-based drought rules descend from the emergency measures negotiated during the 1960s drought. The FFMP conditions all three classes of operating decisions — the NYC and NJ diversions, the downstream release targets, and flood-mitigation releases — on one system state: combined storage in the three NYC reservoirs, compared daily against seasonally varying boundary curves. The boundary curves partition combined storage into seven operating zones: three flood-operation zones (L1a-L1c) at the highest storage levels, a normal zone (L2), and three drought stages (L3, drought watch; L4, drought warning; L5, drought emergency). Each boundary curve traces an annual cycle with a reduced autumn-winter storage target (a seasonal void carried for flood mitigation), a spring rise timed to snowmelt refill, and a summer plateau; the flood-zone curves reach full capacity around June 1, which the FFMP treats as an essential refill objective.

Within the conservation zones (L1c through L5), each reservoir makes directed daily minimum releases according to seasonal release schedules. The FFMP's release tables (Tables 4a-4g of the Operations Plan) express each schedule as a multiple of the reservoir's base release rate (122.8, 64.63, and 48.47 MGD at Cannonsville, Pepacton, and Neversink), with multipliers that increase with storage zone and forecast water availability. Beyond these scheduled minimums, the reservoirs make compensating releases sized, with time-of-travel lags, to maintain the Montague flow target and to support the Trenton flow objective.

On entry to the drought stages, the FFMP curtails deliveries and lowers flow targets in negotiated steps: the NYC diversion is reduced to 0.85, 0.70, and 0.65 of its 800 MGD allocation in L3, L4, and L5; the NJ diversion to 0.90 and 0.80 of its allocation in L4 and L5; and the Montague and Trenton targets are reduced by stage-specific factors. These stepped curtailments trade delivery and flow-target performance against the protection of remaining storage. In the flood-operation zones, the opposite lever operates: the L1a and L1b zones carry enhanced release schedules, and an additional drawdown release evacuates storage in excess of the seasonal void target, with all scheduled releases capped at each reservoir's maximum combined discharge rate (4,200, 2,400, and 3,400 cfs at Cannonsville, Pepacton, and Neversink).

Decree accounting treats the diversion allocations as running averages rather than daily caps: NYC's 800 MGD allocation limits the annual running average of diversion (reset each June 1), and NJ's 100 MGD allocation limits the monthly average, so daily diversions may exceed the flat allocation rate against allowance accrued by prior under-use. This accounting convention matters for the delivery objectives of Section 3.2.2.

The FFMP's parameterized rules — release schedules, curtailment factors, flow-target factors, and storage-zone boundaries — are negotiated constants within a fixed rule structure. Because any change to NYC's Delaware operations requires unanimous consent of the five Decree parties, adaptations must remain legible as renegotiation-scale perturbations of the negotiated compromise, a constraint the decision space of Section 3.2.1 is designed to respect.

### 2.3 Pywr-DRB Simulation of Current Operations

All policy evaluations use Pywr-DRB, an open-source daily water-availability and drought-risk model of the basin built on the Pywr simulation framework (Hamilton et al., 2024). Pywr-DRB represents the four NYC reservoirs and the major lower-basin reservoirs, the FFMP rule structure of Section 2.2 with its storage zones, seasonal release schedules, drought curtailments, and flood-mitigation releases, the Montague and Trenton flow targets with their time-of-travel lags, and the NYC and NJ deliveries under the Decree's running-average accounting.

During optimization we use a trimmed configuration of the model in which the non-NYC reservoirs are simulated once under their default rules and their releases are supplied to the trimmed model as fixed boundary inflows, so that each function evaluation solves only the NYC-relevant portion of the network. This simplification is admissible because the decision variables (Section 3.2.1) alter only NYC operations and no lower-basin operating rule in the model consults an NYC state variable. Because the boundary releases are policy-independent, they are computed once per streamflow realization and reused for every policy evaluated on that realization. The deep uncertainty re-evaluation (Section 3.4) also uses the trimmed configuration, with one boundary-release simulation pass computed per realization under that ensemble's perturbed forcing, so the boundary releases remain consistent with the forcing of every re-evaluation scenario. The full model is used only to generate the boundary releases and to simulate the status-quo baseline policy on the historical record. Verification that the trimmed configuration reproduces full-model objective values under matched inputs is reported in Text S1 in Supporting Information S1.

### 3.1 Scenario Designs for the Search Ensemble

#### 3.1.1 Definition of the Three Scenario Designs

We compare three constructions of the search ensemble, all drawn from a single stationary population and all evaluated at matched ensemble size and record length except where a design cannot be matched by construction (Table 1). A survey of search-ensemble constructions in the water resources policy-search literature is provided in Text S11 in Supporting Information S1.

The Historical design (HIST) simulates the reconstructed historical record — 77 water years (October 1945 through September 2022) of basin inflows reconstructed from gauged records at all model inflow locations (Hamilton et al., 2024; Amestoy et al., 2026) — as one continuous realization. It represents prevailing applied practice, in which policies are optimized directly over the historical record (Giuliani et al., 2016; Herman et al., 2020). Because it cannot be matched in ensemble size or function-evaluation budget, it is reported as a reference rather than entered into the controlled contrast, and because its single realization resolves the reliability objectives below their own sampling noise (Table 3 note), its reliability comparisons are read as illustrative.

The Probabilistic Sampling design (PS) consists of $N$ realizations of length $L$ years drawn i.i.d. from the stationary stochastic generator (Section 3.1.2) and held fixed for the entire search. This is the standard ensemble construction of the regional water-supply planning literature (Quinn et al., 2017; Zatarain Salazar et al., 2017) and the reference against which designed selection is judged.

The Hazard Filling design (HF) is the proposed construction. It selects $N$ realizations of length $L$ years from a large candidate pool by space-filling coverage of the hydrologic hazard space, using the selection procedure of Section 3.1.4. It is constructed from the same stationary generator as PS at the same $N$ and $L$, so the two designs differ only in the rule that determines which realizations enter the search ensemble (Section 3.1.2). The contrast between PS and HF is the direct test of the first research question.

Both matched designs use $N = 100$ realizations of $L = 10$ years, amounting to 1,000 scenario-years per function evaluation. Matching $N$ and $L$ is required for the controlled contrast: if $L$ differed across designs, the selection rule would be confounded with record length, and at fixed per-evaluation cost $N$ and $L$ trade off directly. The value $L = 10$ years exceeds the duration of the 1960s drought of record (four to five years) plus its onset and recovery, so a design-basis event fits within a single realization and duration-type hazard descriptors are not truncation-limited. The value $N = 100$ is bounded below by the stratification requirement of the hazard-filling selection, whose anchor points partition each selection axis into $N$ strata (Section 3.1.4), and bounded above, at fixed candidate-pool size, by the loss of tail representation as additional selections increasingly draw from the dense center of the pool (Text S4 in Supporting Information S1). The statistical adequacy of this per-evaluation sample rests on the annual-unit aggregation described in Section 3.2.3.

The stationary population underlying all three designs is the flow model of the prevailing water-supply optimization literature (Herman et al., 2014; Trindade et al., 2017; Zatarain Salazar et al., 2017; Gold et al., 2023). Hazard variation within it arises entirely from natural variability: some 10-year realizations contain severe multi-year droughts and most do not. Restricting the search population in this way isolates the first research question, which concerns the rule that selects realizations from a fixed generating process, without confounding that rule with a change in the generating process itself. The deeply uncertain climate-forcing space enters the study only through the re-evaluation ensemble (Section 3.4.1), which makes the re-evaluation a test of generalization to conditions absent from search. Hazard-oriented selection within a stationary population has precedent in one-dimensional streamflow stratification and weighted-bootstrap drought amplification (Herman et al., 2016; Zatarain Salazar et al., 2017).

#### 3.1.2 Stochastic Streamflow Generation and the Candidate Pool

Daily multi-site streamflow realizations are generated in two stages. Monthly flows at each model inflow location are generated with the Cholesky-based synthesis of Kirsch et al. (2013), which preserves cross-site and month-to-month correlation, and the monthly flows are disaggregated to daily flows by nearest-neighbor resampling of historical daily flow patterns (Nowak et al., 2010); we refer to the combination as the Kirsch-Nowak generator. The generator is fitted to the reconstructed historical record spanning water years 1946 through 2022 and run without climate perturbation. Generation mechanics, the deterministic seed architecture, and the statistical validation of generated flows are documented in Text S2 in Supporting Information S1.

Both matched designs draw from this one generator; the distinction between them is the mechanism of selection. A design can generate arbitrarily many independent realizations, but it cannot instruct the generator to produce a realization at a prescribed drought severity, because hazard coordinates are emergent properties of a realized sequence and are computable only after the sequence exists. A design that targets hazard coverage must therefore select realizations from a finite set of already-generated sequences, and the nearest-neighbor assignment step of Section 3.1.4 is a consequence of this fact. Generators purpose-built to emit sequences with prescribed drought characteristics exist (Zaniolo et al., 2023, 2024); we do not pursue this alternative because it forgoes the simulation-free scalability of selection from a pool and requires committing to a small number of discrete, analyst-chosen event types.

The HF design draws on a candidate pool $\mathcal{C}$ of $P = 10^6$ realizations sampled independently from the stationary generator. The pool size is set by a nested sizing diagnostic, because the resolution of the severe corners of the hazard space improves with $P$ (Section 3.1.4). Generating pool members involves no system simulation, so the pool is inexpensive to enlarge relative to the cost of search, and no pool realization is ever simulated before selection. Only each realization's hazard coordinates and its generation seed are retained, and the $N$ selected members are regenerated exactly on demand from a deterministic, globally indexed random-stream architecture (Text S2 in Supporting Information S1), which is what makes pools of order $10^6$ members tractable.

The requirement that the candidate pool be sampled independently, rather than by a structured design such as a Latin hypercube, is essential to the validity of the comparison. A uniform random subset of size $N$ taken from an i.i.d. sample has exactly the joint distribution of $N$ fresh independent draws. This distributional equivalence is what makes PS the exact statistical control for HF: the two designs differ only in the rule that selects $N$ members from the same population law, so any difference in re-evaluated robustness is attributable to that rule alone.

#### 3.1.3 Hazard Metric Formulations

Every ensemble scenario is a disjoint, non-overlapping $L$-year realization. Each simulation begins from an initial storage of 80 percent of capacity, the Pywr-DRB default initial condition lying within the reservoirs' normal operating range, and the first six months of each realization are excluded from all metrics as an initialization period that attenuates the influence of this assumption. Six months is also the accumulation period the SSI-6 drought index (below) requires before its first defined value, so hazard metrics and objectives (Section 3.2.2) are defined on the identical window. A drought event that would straddle a realization boundary is truncated by it; the choice of $L$ bounds this truncation (Section 3.1.1), and event-scale severity is additionally captured at re-evaluation, where records are longer.

Hazard coordinates are computed on the aggregate inflow to the three NYC reservoirs before any system simulation. Let $q_x(t)$ denote the daily aggregate NYC inflow of candidate realization $x$, and let $z_x(\tau)$ denote the corresponding monthly series of the six-month Standardized Streamflow Index (SSI-6), computed with a gamma distribution fitted once per calendar month to the historical record (McKee et al., 1993; Vicente-Serrano et al., 2012). Eight candidate hazard descriptors are computed for every realization: five drought descriptors derived from run theory applied to the SSI-6 series, and three flood descriptors derived from a peaks-over-threshold analysis of the daily flows.

Drought events are identified from the SSI-6 series with a threshold-based definition with event pooling. An event begins in the first month that $z_x$ falls below zero, is retained as a drought event only if $z_x$ reaches $-1$ during the event, and terminates only after $z_x$ remains non-negative for three consecutive months. Let $E = \{\tau_{\mathrm{on}}, \ldots, \tau_{\mathrm{end}}\}$ denote the months of an event and $\tau_{\mathrm{pk}}$ the month of its minimum index value. The event's deficit volume $D$ and peak depth $S$ are

$$
D(E) \;=\; -\sum_{\tau \in E} \min\big(z_x(\tau),\, 0\big),
\qquad
S(E) \;=\; -\min_{\tau \in E}\, z_x(\tau),
\tag{1}
$$

in units of SSI-months and standard deviations respectively, and the event's duration is $T(E) = |E|$ months. The onset rate $R_{\mathrm{on}}$ and recovery rate $R_{\mathrm{rec}}$ normalize the peak depth by the time taken to reach and to recover from it,

$$
R_{\mathrm{on}}(E) \;=\; \frac{S(E)}{\tau_{\mathrm{pk}} - \tau_{\mathrm{on}} + 1},
\qquad
R_{\mathrm{rec}}(E) \;=\; \frac{S(E)}{\tau_{\mathrm{end}} - \tau_{\mathrm{pk}} + 1}.
\tag{2}
$$

When a realization contains more than one qualifying event, the controlling event $E^{*} = \arg\max_{E} D(E)$, the event with the largest deficit volume and the operationally binding event under fixed initial storage, is scored. All five drought descriptors are zero for a realization with no qualifying event.

The flood descriptors are computed from the daily flows relative to the high-flow threshold $u$, fixed at the 95th percentile of the historical record's daily flow. The critical pulse $P$ of a realization is the exceedance run, the maximal set of consecutive days with $q_x(t) > u$, that contains the realization's maximum daily flow at day $t_{\mathrm{max}}$, so exactly one pulse is scored per realization and no declustering parameter is required. With $\bar{q}$ the mean daily flow of the historical record, the peak magnitude $M$, pulse duration $T_P$, and rise rate $R_{\mathrm{rise}}$ are

$$
M \;=\; \frac{q_x(t_{\mathrm{max}})}{\bar{q}},
\qquad
T_P \;=\; |P|,
\qquad
R_{\mathrm{rise}} \;=\; \frac{1}{\bar{q}} \max_{t \in P,\; t \le t_{\mathrm{max}}} \big( q_x(t) - q_x(t-1) \big).
\tag{3}
$$

The SSI distribution fits, the flood threshold $u$, and the normalizing mean $\bar{q}$ are all fixed once on the historical record and reused for every realization, so hazard coordinates are directly comparable across the candidate pool, the realized search ensembles, and the Historical reference. Additional descriptor details are given in Text S3 in Supporting Information S1.

The hazard space has two scope limitations. First, the descriptors are defined on aggregate NYC inflow only. The flows that decide the Montague, Trenton, and flood-gauge objectives include unregulated tributary contributions that the hazard coordinates do not resolve, and a supporting analysis correlates the selected ensemble's hazard coordinates against realized stress at the downstream objective sites (Text S3 in Supporting Information S1). Second, the descriptors characterize a single controlling event per realization, so a realization containing two moderate droughts is indistinguishable from a realization containing one; extending the descriptor set to event frequency and timing is left to future work.

The descriptor set is screened for redundancy on the candidate pool before selection, following the index-redundancy logic of Olden and Poff (2003). Degenerate descriptors with near-zero spread are dropped, and near-duplicate pairs, defined by absolute Spearman rank correlation of at least 0.95, are pruned to a single canonical member so that one hazard concept cannot enter the selection distance twice under two names. On this population the screen retains all eight candidate descriptors. The largest pairwise rank correlation, 0.88 between deficit volume and duration, falls below the pruning threshold, is stable across pool sizes from $2 \times 10^3$ to $10^6$ members, and the retained set is insensitive to tightening the threshold to 0.90. Six of the retained descriptors serve as the selection axes: deficit volume, peak depth, onset rate, and recovery rate on the drought side, and peak magnitude and pulse duration on the flood side. The two remaining descriptors are excluded from the selection distance: drought duration as the most redundant member of the retained set (rank correlation 0.87 with deficit volume, with a quasi-discrete, months-valued tail) and flood rise rate as the flood descriptor most correlated with the retained flood pair (rank correlation 0.60 with peak magnitude). Limiting the dimension of the selection space matters because the selection's reach into the severe corners of the hazard space weakens as axes are added at fixed pool size (Section 3.1.4). The full redundancy structure of the descriptor set, together with a selection-invariance analysis quantifying the weighting that correlated axes induce in the selection distance, is reported in Text S3 in Supporting Information S1.

#### 3.1.4 Space-Filling Scenario Selection in Hazard Space

The hazard-filling construction selects the $N$ members of the search ensemble so that they cover the six-dimensional selection space of Section 3.1.3 in absolute, range-scaled magnitude units. Each selection axis $a$ is scaled to the unit interval by its robust range across the candidate pool, using the per-axis 1st and 99th percentiles $h_a^{(1)}$ and $h_a^{(99)}$,

$$
\tilde{h}_a(x) \;=\; \min\!\Big(1, \; \max\!\Big(0, \; \frac{h_a(x) - h_a^{(1)}}{h_a^{(99)} - h_a^{(1)}} \Big)\Big),
\tag{4}
$$

with members beyond the percentile bounds clipped to the faces of the unit hypercube, so that no single axis dominates distances while the spacing within each axis remains proportional to physical magnitude. Robust percentile bounds, rather than the sample extremes, make the selection geometry a stable functional of the underlying population. The sample minimum and maximum of a right-skewed hazard metric are non-convergent extreme order statistics, so a full-range scaling would grow with the pool size $P$, vary materially across independent pool constructions, and allow a single outlier to compress the bulk of the population into a corner of the hypercube. The clipped mass is bounded at one percent per axis tail, and a sensitivity sweep over the bound settings confirms the choice empirically: full-range scaling degrades realized coverage roughly threefold through fixation on outliers, while coverage and tail representation vary smoothly across nearby robust-percentile settings (Text S4 in Supporting Information S1).

The selection then proceeds in two steps. First, $N$ anchor points $\mathbf{u}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{u}_N$ are drawn as a Latin hypercube sample in the scaled hazard space $[0,1]^m$ with $m = 6$. Second, each anchor is assigned to its nearest unselected candidate realization in Euclidean distance,

$$
x_{(i)} \;=\; \underset{x \,\in\, \mathcal{C} \setminus \{x_{(1)}, \ldots, x_{(i-1)}\}}{\arg\min} \; \big\lVert \tilde{\mathbf{h}}(x) - \mathbf{u}_i \big\rVert_2,
\qquad i = 1, \ldots, N,
\tag{5}
$$

and the selected set $\{x_{(1)}, \ldots, x_{(N)}\}$ is the HF search ensemble. The procedure is deterministic given its anchor seed, involves no iterative optimization of a coverage criterion, and has no tuning parameters. Because Latin hypercube sampling stratifies every axis marginally regardless of dimension, the procedure's guarantee is stratified marginal coverage with tail representation on each selection axis. Joint coverage is reported descriptively against a random subset of the same size and dimension rather than claimed as uniform (Text S4 in Supporting Information S1). An optimal one-to-one assignment of anchors to candidates is indistinguishable from the sequential nearest-neighbor rule of Equation 5 on every coverage metric, so the simpler rule is retained (Text S4 in Supporting Information S1).

The realized coverage that the procedure achieves is set jointly by the pool size and the dimension of the selection space. The expected distance from an anchor to its nearest candidate scales as $P^{-1/m}$ on $m$ axes, so at fixed $P$ each added axis increases the anchor-to-candidate distances and pulls selected members back toward the dense center of the population. In the supporting diagnostics, the mean assignment distance roughly triples between four-axis and eight-axis selection on the same pool, with per-axis tail representation falling accordingly (Text S4 in Supporting Information S1). The design therefore restricts the selection space to the six axes of Section 3.1.3 and sets $P = 10^6$ from a nested sizing diagnostic, in which the selection is repeated on nested prefixes of a single large pool, each an exact i.i.d. sample of its intermediate size under the deterministic seed architecture (Text S2 in Supporting Information S1). At the campaign configuration, every selection axis places at least 31 percent of selected members above the pool's 90th percentile, roughly three times the 10 percent share that i.i.d. sampling delivers, and this realized enrichment is re-measured on every campaign construction as a build diagnostic (Text S4 in Supporting Information S1).

Scaling the selection axes by absolute magnitude serves the construction's purpose: a search ensemble whose members span the full range of hydrologic conditions the generating process can produce. The hazard marginals of a stationary generator are strongly right-skewed, so most candidate realizations cluster near the center of the hazard space and few occupy the severe corners. Filling each axis's range uniformly therefore distributes the selected members across the entire spectrum of drought and flood conditions, from benign to severe. Relative to independent sampling, this coverage necessarily over-represents rare severe conditions. That consequence is accepted deliberately: severe conditions are where reservoir policies are decided, and no across-ensemble aggregation operator can recover information about severe sequences that the ensemble does not contain. Uniform coverage of a stress space, rather than probability-faithful sampling of it, follows the decision-scaling and scenario-neutral traditions, in which climate stress spaces are sampled uniformly to map system response rather than to estimate expectations (Brown et al., 2012; Culley et al., 2016; Fowler et al., 2024), and severity over-weighting during vulnerability assessment has direct precedent (Herman et al., 2016).

The two matched designs consequently present different scenario compositions to the search. PS presents the generator's own distribution, while HF presents a set spanning the full hazard range, whose composition differs from the generating distribution, so objectives aggregated over the HF ensemble weight severe conditions more heavily than their generating frequency. This compositional difference changes which policies the optimizer retains, which is why search objectives are never compared across designs (Section 3.2.3) and why the common re-evaluation ensemble of Section 3.4 is the only point of cross-design comparison. Whether hazard-space coverage during search yields policies that are more robust under deeply uncertain re-evaluation, or instead tailors policies to conditions the re-evaluation does not reward, is the empirical content of the first research question.

### 3.2 Many-Objective Problem Formulation

We pose the re-optimization of NYC's reservoir operations as the many-objective problem

$$
\min_{\mathbf{x} \in \mathcal{X}} \; \mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x}; E) = \big(J_1(\mathbf{x}; E), \ldots, J_8(\mathbf{x}; E)\big)
\quad \text{s.t.} \quad c_{\mathrm{fld}}(\mathbf{x}) \le 0, \;\; c_{\mathrm{rel}}(\mathbf{x}; E) \le 0,
\tag{6}
$$

where $\mathbf{x}$ is the decision vector parameterizing the FFMP rule structure (Section 3.2.1), $E$ is the search ensemble of streamflow realizations defined by the active scenario design (Section 3.1), and $J_1, \ldots, J_8$ are the objectives aggregated over $E$ (Sections 3.2.2 and 3.2.3, Table 3). Maximization objectives are negated so that all objectives are posed to the algorithm in minimization form. Two feasibility constraints apply. The flood-ordering constraint $c_{\mathrm{fld}}$ requires the effective L1b release schedule not to exceed the effective L1a schedule at any reservoir; it is computed from decision-variable arithmetic alone, so infeasible vectors are discarded under constraint-dominance before any simulation is spent on them. The reliability-floor constraint $c_{\mathrm{rel}}$ requires NYC delivery reliability (objective 1 of Table 3) of at least 0.50 on the search ensemble, encoding the stakeholder requirement that a policy failing in half of all years is unacceptable regardless of its other objective values; it is evaluated after simulation from the computed objective vector.

#### 3.2.2 Objective Definitions

We consider eight objectives spanning the range of stakeholder priorities encoded in the basin's governance (Table 3): NYC water supply (delivery reliability and deficit severity), the Decree flow obligation at Montague (flow reliability and deficit severity), the Trenton flow objective, downstream flood exposure, NYC reservoir storage, and NJ delivery reliability. Trenton flow reliability and NJ delivery reliability give the lower-basin Decree interests direct representation, so the search can expose conflicts between NYC and its co-equal Decree parties (Trindade et al., 2017; Hadjimichael et al., 2020). A redundancy screen over constraint-feasible policies confirmed that no objective in the set is collinear with another (Text S5 in Supporting Information S1).

All objectives are computed on an evaluation window that begins six months after the start of each simulation, the same window on which the hazard metrics are defined (Section 3.1.3). Objectives are computed from weekly aggregates of the daily simulation output, the accounting interval of Decree compliance: the delivery objectives aggregate daily deliveries and entitlements to weekly volumes, and the flow objectives use weekly mean flows.

Compliance is scored against the static, court-mandated quantities: the 800 MGD NYC diversion allocation, the 1,131.05 MGD (1,750 cfs) Montague flow target, the 100 MGD NJ diversion allocation, and the 1,938.95 MGD (3,000 cfs) Trenton equivalent-flow objective. The FFMP's drought-curtailed targets are never used as the scoring reference, because the deficit of interest is the shortfall below the court-mandated obligation: a policy scored against targets that its own drought stages have lowered would conceal exactly the shortfall being measured. Conversely, demand in excess of the court-mandated allocation is not counted as a shortfall. Because the Decree limits the running average of diversion rather than any single day's diversion (Section 2.2), each day's delivery target is the realizable entitlement

$$
E_t \;=\; \min\big(d_t, \; A_t\big),
\tag{8}
$$

where $d_t$ is that day's demand and $A_t$ is the running-average allowance accrued to it, reconstructed at the static allocation (with NYC's allowance resetting annually on June 1 and NJ's monthly) so that a policy's own curtailments cannot lower it. The entitlement honors a demand peak whenever prior under-use has accrued the allowance for it, and counts no shortfall against demand beyond the accrued allowance.

The four reliability objectives (1, 3, 5, and 8 in Table 3) measure the frequency with which an obligation is met, the standard reliability form for water resources systems (Hashimoto et al., 1982). For the delivery objectives,

$$
R \;=\; \frac{1}{W} \sum_{w=1}^{W} \mathbf{1}\!\left[\, V_w \ge 0.99\, V^{*}_w \,\right],
\tag{9}
$$

where $V_w$ and $V^{*}_w$ are the delivered and entitlement volumes of week $w$ and 0.99 is the weekly satisfaction factor; the flow reliabilities are analogous, with the weekly mean flow compared directly against the static target. Equation 9 is the reliability's whole-record form, given for orientation: as objectives, the reliabilities are aggregated by the annual-unit scheme of Section 3.2.3, in which a year fails when at least $k$ of its weeks violate the weekly condition inside Equation 9, and the objective is the frequency of non-failure years (Table 3). NYC delivery reliability measures the city's supply security, Montague flow reliability measures compliance with the Decree's compensating-release obligation, Trenton flow reliability measures attainment of the flow objective that repels the salt front, and NJ delivery reliability carries New Jersey's independent diversion interest. Trenton flow reliability serves as the salinity objective deliberately: the Trenton flow objective exists to hold the salt front below the Philadelphia and Camden intakes, which makes the two physically redundant, and the flow form avoids reliance on a statistical salinity emulator in the extreme droughts where objective values are decided. The two deficit objectives (2 and 4) measure the severity of the failures that the corresponding reliability objectives count. The weekly deficit, in percent of the obligation, is $\delta_w = 100\,(V^{*}_w - V_w)^{+}/V^{*}$ for NYC delivery and $\delta_w = 100\,(T - \bar{q}_w)^{+}/T$ for Montague flow, with $T$ the static target and $\bar{q}_w$ the weekly mean flow. Severity is summarized by the conditional value-at-risk at the 90 percent level,

$$
\mathrm{CVaR}_{90} \;=\; \operatorname{mean}\big\{\, \delta_w : \delta_w \ge Q_{90}(\delta) \,\big\},
\tag{10}
$$

the mean of the worst decile of weekly deficits, where $Q_{90}$ is the 90th percentile of the weekly deficit series (Rockafellar and Uryasev, 2000). The single worst week, common in earlier formulations, is a high-variance, low-information signal (Quinn et al., 2017); CVaR$_{90}$ preserves the tail-risk focus while averaging enough weeks to be reproducible across realizations (Löhndorf, 2016; Fairbrother et al., 2022). As objectives, the CVaR$_{90}$ is computed within each annual unit and summarized across units by the worst first-percentile unit (Section 3.2.3, Table 3).

Downstream flood exposure (objective 6) is measured at three reservoir-tailwater gauges with National Weather Service (NWS) flood stages (Hale Eddy, Fishs Eddy, and Bridgeville) as the mean annual flood exceedance,

$$
J_{\mathrm{fld}} \;=\; \frac{1}{Y} \sum_{t} \max_{g \in \mathcal{G}} \big( h_g(t) - h_g^{\mathrm{minor}} \big)^{+},
\tag{11}
$$

where $h_g(t)$ is the river stage at gauge $g$ on day $t$, $h_g^{\mathrm{minor}}$ is that gauge's NWS minor flood stage, and $Y$ is the number of evaluation-window years, giving units of ft-days per year invariant to record length. This magnitude-weighted form resolves policy differences that a flood-day count collapses onto a few integer values, tracks observed annual flood magnitude, and retains a physical integrand rather than monetized damage, whose expectation-based objectives can mask severe events (Quinn et al., 2017). The maximum across gauges prevents one basin-wide event from being counted three times. Flood-day counts at the NWS minor and major stages and at the FFMP action stage are retained as reported diagnostics.

The NYC storage objective (7) is computed from daily aggregate storage in the three NYC reservoirs, in percent of combined capacity: its annual metric is the annual minimum of daily storage, summarized across annual units by the first percentile (Section 3.2.3, Table 3). It guards the drought reserve on which every other obligation ultimately draws, and a low percentile of the annual minima is preferred to the whole-record single-day minimum, which is dominated by one drought event per record (Quinn et al., 2017).

### 3.3 Many-Objective Search Configuration

All searches use the multi-master Borg multiobjective evolutionary algorithm (MM Borg; Hadka and Reed, 2013, 2015), whose epsilon-dominance archiving, adaptive operator selection, and randomized restarts have proven reliable across many-objective water resources applications (Reed et al., 2013; Zatarain Salazar et al., 2016, 2017). Both matched designs run to an equal function-evaluation budget of 500,000 NFE, within the range used for comparable reservoir policy-search problems (Quinn et al., 2017; Bartholomew and Kwakkel, 2020). Because $N$ and $L$ are common, the per-evaluation simulation cost is identical across designs, so equal NFE and equal simulated scenario-years coincide and ensemble composition is not confounded with search effort. Each search runs on the Purdue Anvil system as four islands of 254 evaluator ranks (1,021 MPI ranks on eight nodes), 125,000 evaluations per island, with the calibrated epsilon-dominance precisions of Table 3 shared by every search. The parallelization measurements that size this configuration are reported in Text S8 in Supporting Information S1.

Each design's search is run with $S = 2$ independent random seeds to account for the stochastic initialization and operator dynamics of evolutionary search, and the seed archives are merged and non-dominated sorted into a single Pareto-approximate set per design (Hadka and Reed, 2015; Zatarain Salazar et al., 2017). This merged set is the object carried to re-evaluation; no per-seed results are analyzed, and the reliability of MM Borg's epsilon-dominance archiving and randomized restarts across random seed trials is documented elsewhere (Hadka and Reed, 2013, 2015). The HIST reference runs the same seed count and merging.

Runtime archive snapshots are recorded every 2,500 evaluations per island. Search progress is monitored through runtime epsilon-progress and hypervolume diagnostics computed against reference sets constructed separately per design, because objective values computed on different search ensembles are not commensurable and a reference set merged across designs would score each design against a frontier it helped define (Zatarain Salazar et al., 2017; Text S7 in Supporting Information S1; these diagnostics are being finalized). These quantities are read purely as convergence diagnostics and are never compared across designs. Convergence assessment is not used as a stopping rule, so no design benefits from a tuned termination criterion, and both matched designs execute the identical NFE limit. Because the runtime archive records the approximation set at intermediate NFE levels, the design comparison is recomputable at earlier NFE levels at re-evaluation cost only, which tests whether the design ranking depends on the search length.

### 3.4 Deep Uncertainty Re-Evaluation and Robustness Analysis

#### 3.4.1 The Deeply Uncertain Re-Evaluation Ensemble

Every design's merged Pareto-approximate set is stress tested on a single common re-evaluation ensemble, following the deep uncertainty (DU) re-evaluation approach of the robust-decision-making lineage (Kasprzyk et al., 2013; Trindade et al., 2017), using the trimmed model configuration of Section 2.3 with the policy-independent boundary releases simulated once per realization. All cross-design comparisons are made there. The re-evaluation ensemble is never used during any search and is never the source of any search ensemble: it is generated from a reserved seed domain disjoint from that of the candidate pool, and any collision between the search-side and test-side seed domains is treated as a hard error, which guards against testing on scenarios the search has seen (Bonham et al., 2024).

The re-evaluation ensemble is constructed to span a deeply uncertain climate-forcing envelope rather than to represent a probability distribution. The forcing is a monthly change factor applied to the generator's fitted moments, parameterized as a low-order harmonic in the log change factor whose amplitudes govern annual volume, seasonal amplitude, and the snowmelt-shoulder shape, with amplitude ranges taken from a 54-member CMIP6 multimodel ensemble of monthly streamflow change profiles (Text S6 in Supporting Information S1). The adjustment preserves each month's coefficient of variation, so the forcing perturbs the mean seasonal profile without independently altering relative variability (an independent variance axis was evaluated and excluded on hazard-coverage grounds, Text S6 in Supporting Information S1). Each state-of-the-world (SOW) is one Latin hypercube point in the three-dimensional amplitude space, sampled over the full empirical range of the CMIP6-fitted amplitudes widened by a 25 percent margin, an envelope deliberately wider than any variation the search ensembles contain. Each SOW is crossed with stochastic realizations that sample natural variability within that state.

The ensemble comprises 1,000 SOWs with 25 realizations per SOW and 50-year records, for 25,000 realizations and 1.25 million scenario-years. The SOW count fixes the Monte Carlo resolution of any reported satisficing fraction at plus or minus 1.6 percentage points in the worst case, the standard error of a binomial fraction estimated over 1,000 SOWs. Each SOW's 1,225 metric-bearing annual units match the sample at which the tail operators of Section 3.2.3 stabilize, so the within-SOW performance estimate is resolved at least as well as one search evaluation. The 50-year records, five times the search-record length, additionally test sustained operation, because consecutive droughts are experienced on carried-over storage and the running-average delivery accounting matures, neither of which a terminating 10-year realization can express. Where the re-evaluation ensemble itself is placed in the hazard space, its hazard coordinates are computed on disjoint, October-aligned 10-year sub-windows, so they are commensurable with the candidate pool's window convention. The adequacy of the SOW sample is verified after re-evaluation by SOW-subsample stability of the design rankings, computed from the stored re-evaluation output at no additional simulation cost; the adequacy of the within-state sample rests on the 1,225-unit pooling above, and the Monte Carlo noise of the per-state objective estimates is additionally measured from the incumbent policy's re-evaluation as part of the tolerance calibration of Section 3.4.3. This construction, a Latin hypercube sample of SOWs crossed with stochastic realizations, is the standard deep uncertainty re-evaluation design of the robustness literature (Kasprzyk et al., 2013; Trindade et al., 2017; Bartholomew and Kwakkel, 2020; Quinn et al., 2020; Gold et al., 2022).

Two properties of the re-evaluation ensemble govern the interpretation of the comparison. First, the search ensembles are drawn from the unperturbed stationary generator, whereas the re-evaluation ensemble spans a forced climate envelope that no search ensemble contains. The re-evaluation therefore measures whether hazard-space coverage of the generator's natural variability produces policies that generalize to conditions absent from search, a stronger test than re-evaluating on the search distribution, and one that keeps the re-evaluation ensemble structurally distinct from both matched designs so that it favors neither. Second, no robustness value reported in this study is an expectation, because under deep uncertainty there is no probability measure over the forcing space (Lamontagne et al., 2018). A satisficing fraction computed over the re-evaluation ensemble is a coverage-weighted count over a designed exploration of the forcing envelope, and it is reported as such. The cross-design comparison is commensurable because the re-evaluation ensemble is identical across designs, not because it is probability-faithful.

No claim is made that the re-evaluation ensemble is scenario-neutral — no experimental design is (Quinn et al., 2020) — and all design rankings are conditional on this one reference ensemble. Composition-sensitivity analyses, in which hazard-restricted and envelope-restricted subsets of the re-evaluation ensemble are re-scored to probe whether the ranking depends on the region of the forcing space emphasized, are reported in Text S10 in Supporting Information S1. The construction inherits the historical record's interannual wet-dry persistence, so persistence is stressed in neither the search population nor the re-evaluation ensemble, and all claims are scoped to hold under historical-level interannual persistence (Text S2 in Supporting Information S1). NYC and NJ demands are held constant at the Decree maxima, the institutionally binding upper bounds on deliveries under the governance structure of Section 2.1, so no demand-growth axis enters either search or re-evaluation.

#### 3.4.2 Satisficing Robustness

The primary robustness measure for the first research question is satisficing robustness under the domain criterion (Starr, 1962): the fraction of sampled states of the world in which a policy satisfies all performance criteria. This is the standard satisficing measure of the regional water-supply planning literature (Herman et al., 2014, 2015; Trindade et al., 2017; Gold et al., 2022, 2023) and the fastest-converging robustness family in ensemble-size testing (Bonham et al., 2024).

The performance statistic inside this and every other robustness and regret measure is the optimization objective itself, recomputed per state of the world: what differs between search and re-evaluation is the ensemble over which the statistic is computed and the aggregation across states, never the definition of the statistic. This is the standard construction of the robustness literature, in which the performance measure entering a robustness calculation is the search objective re-evaluated in each state (Herman et al., 2014, 2015; Trindade et al., 2017; McPhail et al., 2018; Gold et al., 2023). Because the re-evaluation ensemble is nested, with $R$ stochastic realizations inside each of the $N_\theta$ deeply uncertain states, the per-state objective values are obtained by pooling each state's realizations' annual units through the objective's $\Phi$/$\Psi$ pair of Equation 12,

$$
J_i(\mathbf{x}, \theta) \;=\; \Psi_{(r,y)}\Big[\, \Phi_{t \in y}\big[\, g_i(t, r) \,\big] \,\Big],
\qquad r = 1, \ldots, R, \;\; y = 1, \ldots, L_{\mathrm{test}} - 1,
\tag{13a}
$$

where the realizations $r$ are those of state $\theta$, so that $J_i(\mathbf{x}, \theta)$ is objective $i$'s value in state $\theta$, in natural units, computed from that state's $R\,(L_{\mathrm{test}} - 1) = 1{,}225$ metric-bearing annual units exactly as one search evaluation computes it from the search ensemble's 900. The pooled sample keeps the tail operators well defined within each state: the per-state first percentile is approximately the twelfth-worst annual unit, the worst-first-percentile-year construction of Quinn et al. (2018) applied per state of the world. The domain criterion is then applied across states,

$$
S(\mathbf{x}) \;=\; \frac{1}{N_{\theta}} \sum_{\theta} \Lambda_\theta(\mathbf{x}),
\qquad
\Lambda_\theta(\mathbf{x}) \;=\; \prod_{i} \mathbf{1}\!\left[\, J_{i}(\mathbf{x}, \theta) \;\text{meets}\; c_i \,\right],
\tag{13b}
$$

where $c_i$ is objective $i$'s satisficing criterion. The state of the world is the unit of the reported fraction, following the regional water-supply planning literature, whose objectives are likewise ensemble statistics per state (Herman et al., 2014; Trindade et al., 2017; Bartholomew and Kwakkel, 2020; Gold et al., 2023); an alternative convention applies the criterion to each realization individually (Hadjimichael et al., 2020; Quinn et al., 2020). We adopt the state-level unit for three reasons. It is the only unit consistent with the absence of a probability measure over the forcing space: natural variability within a state enters through the statistic's own definition, exactly as it does during search, while the states themselves receive a coverage-weighted count, so no separate within-state risk-attitude choice arises or needs to be recorded. It is the unit on which the reported precision is defined, since realizations sharing a forcing point are not independent and the Monte Carlo resolution of Section 3.4.1 is governed by $N_\theta$. And it places the satisficing and regret families (Section 3.4.3) on a common unit.

Re-evaluation scores every policy in each design's merged Pareto-approximate set, so cross-design comparisons operate on the distribution of satisficing fractions across each design's set. The best-attained satisficing fraction summarizes whether a design can find a robust policy at all; because a maximum over a set of policies tends to increase with set size, it is always accompanied by the per-objective satisficing decomposition and the set cardinalities by design. The criteria $c_i$ are stated on the same annual-unit statistics as the objectives; they are anchored on Decree and FFMP quantities wherever one exists and are otherwise conventions descended from stakeholder elicitation practice (Zeff et al., 2014). The unanchored criteria are therefore swept over a common stringency axis, and the design comparison is reported as a difference that holds, or fails to hold, across the range of defensible criteria, because robustness rankings are known to degrade as satisficing criteria become more stringent (Quinn et al., 2020; sweep construction and threshold-margin diagnostics in Text S10 in Supporting Information S1). [Open item: the criterion anchors. Two external goalposts carry over — downstream flood exceedance of 1.17 ft-days per year and the FFMP's 26 percent L5 drought-emergency storage floor (which the incumbent's historical first-percentile annual-minimum storage of 0 percent does not attain). The remaining criteria are provisionally anchored on the stricter side of the incumbent policy's attainment of the same annual-unit statistics on the historical record; final values pend the threshold diagnostic re-run on the per-state substrate.]

Alongside the satisficing fraction, three reference measures are reported, all transformations of the same per-state objective values $J_i(\mathbf{x}, \theta)$. Univariate satisficing gives the per-objective decomposition of Equation 13b on the same unit. The risk-neutral reference is the mean of $J_i(\mathbf{x}, \theta)$ across states, and the risk-averse reference is the worst state's value, the strictest of the available risk attitudes. Both are reported because the choice of robustness metric is known to change robustness rankings (Herman et al., 2015; Giuliani and Castelletti, 2016; McPhail et al., 2018). Agreement among the design rankings induced by these measures and by the regret family of Section 3.4.3 is summarized with Kendall's rank correlation $\tau_b$ (McPhail et al., 2020), and because a robustness scalar can be stable, optimizable, and still misleading (Huang et al., 2025), the raw performance distributions on the re-evaluation ensemble are co-reported alongside every robustness value.

#### 3.4.3 Regret Relative to Current Operations

The second research question asks whether re-optimized policies improve some outcomes without degrading others below the performance of current operations. Answering it requires measuring each policy's downside relative to the incumbent policy in each state of the world rather than on average, because a policy can hold a comfortably positive mean improvement while performing worse than current operations in a substantial minority of futures. The reference is therefore the incumbent 2017 FFMP policy evaluated in the same state of the world. Writing $\sigma_i = +1$ for a maximized objective and $-1$ for a minimized one, the signed advantage of policy $\mathbf{x}$ over the incumbent $\mathbf{b}$ is

$$
D_i(\mathbf{x}, \theta) \;=\; \sigma_i\left[J_i(\mathbf{x}, \theta) - J_i(\mathbf{b}, \theta)\right],
\tag{14}
$$

with $J_i(\cdot, \theta)$ the per-state objective value of Equation 13a, oriented so that positive values always indicate improvement over current operations; the regret family is therefore a transformation of the same per-state objective values as the satisficing family. Regret is the adverse part, $G_i = \max(0, -D_i)$, reported per objective as the mean and the 90th percentile across states of the world; the mean gain $\max(0, D_i)$ is reported alongside, because a policy attains zero regret merely by reproducing the incumbent, which lies within the searched decision space.

A fixed decision alternative evaluated per scenario is an admitted reference within the standard robustness-metric taxonomy (McPhail et al., 2018), the per-objective deviation summarized by a high percentile follows established regret practice (Herman et al., 2015), and the restriction to adverse deviations follows the undesirable-deviations construction (Kwakkel et al., 2016). The measure is equivalently Savage regret on the two-alternative action set the Decree parties face under unanimity, retaining the FFMP or adopting a candidate revision (Savage, 1951). Because the incumbent is external and fixed, the measure does not move when a design is added to or dropped from the comparison. Maximum regret is not reported, because extreme-of-extremes statistics do not converge at attainable ensemble sizes for tail-defined objectives (Bonham et al., 2024). Regret is reported in each objective's own natural units and never aggregated across objectives: ratio normalization is degenerate for this objective set because the incumbent's flood exceedance and both deficit metrics are exactly zero in a share of re-evaluation states of the world (a degeneracy noted previously for near-zero references: Herman et al., 2015), and the published alternatives — normalizing on the span to a per-scenario perfect-foresight optimum, or rescaling over the evaluated alternative set — respectively require one optimization per scenario and couple the scale to the set of designs compared (Cohen et al., 2021; Sunkara et al., 2023).

Cross-objective and cross-design summaries are therefore carried by frequencies, which require no scale. Let $\tau_i$ be a per-objective tolerance. The no-harm frequency

$$
\Pi_\tau(\mathbf{x}) \;=\; \frac{1}{N_\theta}\sum_{\theta} \prod_i \mathbf{1}\!\left[\, D_i(\mathbf{x}, \theta) \ge -\tau_i \,\right]
\tag{15}
$$

is the fraction of states of the world in which no objective is degraded beyond tolerance, and $\Pi_0$ is the frequency of weak Pareto improvement on the incumbent, the condition prior work has applied as a solution filter (Cohen et al., 2021); reporting it as a graded frequency retains the policies whose trade-off against the incumbent is the substance of the question. We also report the per-objective harm frequencies, the mean number of objectives simultaneously degraded, and a Decree-party decomposition in which a party is counted as worse off when any of its objectives is degraded. The party criterion is a disjunction rather than a sum or an average, because under unanimity a loss to one party is not compensable by a gain to another, and compensatory aggregation across actors is known to conceal actor-level failure (Sunkara et al., 2023).

The tolerances are set as $\tau_i = k\,u_i$, where $k$ is a shared multiplier and $u_i = \max(\epsilon_i, \tau_i^{\mathrm{floor}})$ combines objective $i$'s epsilon precision with the noise floor of its per-state objective estimate, measured from the incumbent policy's own re-evaluation. The floor term is required because one $k$ is shared across all eight objectives: an objective whose precision lies below its estimator noise would register degradation on Monte Carlo noise at every tolerance level while the same tolerance sat far outside the noise on every other objective. The answer to the second research question is reported across a sweep of $k$, as the tolerance range over which it holds, for the same reason the satisficing criteria are swept: a single tolerance could manufacture or conceal the result (Quinn et al., 2020). Both the tolerance rule and the sweep bounds are fixed before any candidate-policy regret is inspected, so no anchor is drawn from the distribution under test; the rules and diagnostics are specified in Text S12 in Supporting Information S1.

The satisficing and regret families use different references and therefore discriminate under different conditions. The satisficing criteria are fixed thresholds, so in states of the world where no policy can attain them the domain criterion is zero for every policy and separates nothing, a documented saturation failure mode (Bonham et al., 2024). The regret reference is the incumbent's performance in the same state, which degrades as the forcing does, so the regret measures continue to separate policies in exactly those states. Wherever the two families are compared, both are read off the same policy, and the joint result is presented in the plane they span together with each design's non-dominated frontier within it.

Two regret references are deliberately excluded, and one supporting analysis is added. Best-in-set regret, which references the best-performing policy in each scenario, is design-coupled — removing one design changes every other design's score — and is the slowest-converging metric family at attainable ensemble sizes (Herman et al., 2015; Bonham et al., 2024). Perfect-foresight regret requires one optimization per scenario and does not scale to this study's ensembles; it is cited as motivation only (Cohen et al., 2021). The percent-deviation measure that references a policy's own performance in a baseline state of the world measures sensitivity to assumptions about the future and answers a different question from the second research question (Kasprzyk et al., 2013; Herman et al., 2015). Finally, the re-evaluation states of the world in which no evaluated policy meets the satisficing criteria are identified, so that each design's satisficing fraction is read against the attainable ceiling of the evaluated policy pool; test scenarios unattainable by any policy are a documented feature of deep uncertainty re-evaluation (Shavazipour et al., 2021).

The two measure families jointly operationalize the study's central hypothesis. Prior evidence indicates that robustness gained by emphasizing stressful conditions during search is often paid for in individual scenarios, the price of robustness (Bertsimas and Sim, 2004; Bartholomew and Kwakkel, 2020), and that policies optimized under one scenario distribution and scored under another tend to be dominated by policies optimized under the scoring distribution (Giuliani and Castelletti, 2016). Hazard filling searches under a scenario composition that deliberately differs from the re-evaluation ensemble, so these results predict a penalty for it. The comparison is constructed to detect either outcome: higher satisficing robustness without greater incumbent-relative regret at the stated tolerances, or a measurable price. Because emphasizing severe conditions during search can also skew multi-objective trade-offs toward particular objectives (Huang et al., 2025), the per-objective regret decomposition is examined for objective-selective degradation, and both measure families are decomposed over terciles of the dominant forcing factor to locate any price paid in the benign futures.

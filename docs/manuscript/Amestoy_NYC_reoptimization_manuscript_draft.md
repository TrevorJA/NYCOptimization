## 1. Introduction

*[The literature-positioning paragraphs below are held as a structured outline until the Results are complete. Each bullet corresponds to one planned paragraph; the contribution statement, research questions, and roadmap that follow are drafted prose.]*

- **P1. Motivation.** Water supply systems face growing hydroclimatic stress; operating rules negotiated under assumed stationarity must perform outside their design basis. Many-objective evolutionary search coupled with simulation is the standard tool for exploring rule adaptation and revealing trade-offs among competing uses (Reed et al., 2013; Giuliani et al., 2016; Herman et al., 2020). Every candidate policy is evaluated over a finite scenario set, thousands to millions of times per search, so the composition of that set determines which hydrologic conditions shape the resulting policies. In most applications the search ensemble is inherited rather than designed; the prevailing choices are the historical record and an independent sample from a fitted stochastic generator.

- **P2. Evidence that the search ensemble matters.** Cohen et al. (2021): training-scenario properties determine out-of-sample robustness; high-regret subsets matched full-ensemble performance at roughly one third of the cost, while coarse hydrologic aggregates failed. Zaniolo et al. (2023): the drought type embedded in a training ensemble changes installed infrastructure, technology mix, and the state variables a policy consults. Brodeur et al. (2020): policies calibrated to a single sequence overfit to its event ordering; resampled training sets generalize better. McPhail et al. (2020): changing the scenario set moves robustness values far more than robustness rankings, a null-direction precedent for comparisons whose endpoint is relative performance.

- **P3. The evidence is two-sided.** Eker and Kwakkel (2018): scenarios selected for policy relevance and diversity did not outperform an arbitrary set on robustness or trade-off variety. Giuliani and Castelletti (2016): policies designed under one across-scenario aggregation and scored under another are systematically dominated by policies designed under the scoring measure, a direct warning to any method that alters the scenario distribution during search. Whether a designed search ensemble can outperform a probabilistic one is therefore an open question, and answering it requires an experiment built to detect the effect if it exists and to report its absence if it does not.

- **P4. Three spaces.** Distinguish the input space (generator parameters, climate multipliers, demand factors; a sampled point is a state of the world, SOW, in the robust-decision-making lineage: Kasprzyk et al., 2013; Trindade et al., 2017), the outcome space (simulation outputs; objective values of a policy under a scenario), and, between them, the hazard space, the space of hydrologic hazard metrics computed directly on each realized streamflow sequence before any system simulation (drought severity, duration, intensity; low-flow and high-flow indices). Hazard coordinates are properties of the scenario, not of the simulated response, and are computable for millions of candidate sequences at negligible cost. Nearest construct is the exposure space of the scenario-neutral tradition (Culley et al., 2016; Fowler et al., 2024), but exposure spaces are attribute targets imposed on generator inputs, whereas hazard coordinates are measured on realized sequences.

- **P5. Neither prevailing ensemble is designed to represent stress.** The historical record's hazard content is whatever the observed decades delivered. A probability-faithful generator sample populates the common center of the hazard distribution densely and the severe corners sparsely, though the severe corners are where reservoir policies are decided. Prior hazard control in search ensembles is restricted: one-dimensional, probability-preserving stratification of a stationary pool (Zatarain Salazar et al., 2017); drought properties controlled at generation time at a few discrete analyst-chosen levels (Herman et al., 2016; Zaniolo et al., 2023, 2024); space-filling subsampling of SOW ensembles for post-hoc robustness ranking rather than for search (Bonham et al., 2024). Outcome-informed subset selection for optimization exists but requires simulating every candidate scenario through the system model (Giudici et al., 2020; Hilbers et al., 2019), the dependence that selection in hazard space removes. Related robustness-in-search results: small carried ensembles materially change solutions found (Watson and Kasprzyk, 2017); vulnerability-plus-diversity selection outside the search loop (Carlsen et al., 2016).

- **P6. The open problem.** Bartholomew and Kwakkel (2020) close their comparison of robustness-in-search methods with the problem this study takes up directly: "Research is needed into the selection [of] scenarios which as a set contain the appropriately stressing conditions against which solutions have to be robust, while also capturing the scenarios under which one would like to have near optimal performance."

This study contributes hazard filling, a search-ensemble construction that selects scenarios from a large candidate pool of synthetic streamflow sequences so that the retained members cover the hydrologic hazard space, deliberately over-representing the severe corners relative to their frequency under the generating process. The selection is simulation-free because hazard coordinates are computed on the sequences alone, so the construction scales to candidate pools of order one million sequences and transfers across objective formulations. Problem-driven selection properties such as baseline regret, by contrast, require one perfect-foresight optimization per candidate scenario (Cohen et al., 2021). The selector is deliberately the simplest defensible space-filling design, consisting of Latin hypercube anchors in the hazard space that are each snapped to the nearest unused pool member. We test this construction against the prevailing default, an independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) sample from the same stochastic generator, in a controlled comparison at matched ensemble size, record length, and function-evaluation budget on a real multi-reservoir system. The application is the re-optimization of New York City's Delaware River Basin reservoir operations within the Flexible Flow Management Program (FFMP) rule structure, simulated with the open-source Pywr-DRB model (Hamilton et al., 2024). To our knowledge, this is the first study to test hazard-space scenario selection against probabilistic sampling at matched ensemble size, sequence length, and search budget on a real system, with both designs re-evaluated on a common held-out, deeply uncertain ensemble and with a replication scheme that separates ensemble-construction variance from search variance.

Because a design that deliberately alters the scenario distribution is not commensurable with a probabilistic one on its own search objectives, all cross-design comparison is made in a single way. Every design's Pareto-approximate policies are re-evaluated on one common, held-out re-evaluation ensemble and compared there with a fixed set of robustness metrics (Zatarain Salazar et al., 2017; McPhail et al., 2020). The re-evaluation ensemble is constructed to be deeply uncertain, sampling a wide envelope of climate-forcing perturbations crossed with stochastic realizations. Because the search ensembles are drawn from the unperturbed generator while the re-evaluation ensemble spans a forced climate envelope, the comparison measures whether hazard-space coverage of the natural-variability manifold produces policies that generalize to conditions never presented during search.

We use this design to address three research questions. First, does constructing the search ensemble by hazard-space coverage, rather than by independent sampling from the same stochastic generator, change the robustness of the resulting Pareto-approximate policies under held-out, deeply uncertain re-evaluation? Second, can re-optimizing the parameters of the existing FFMP rule structure improve NYC and basin-wide outcomes, including water supply reliability, Montague and Trenton flow-target compliance, downstream flood exposure, and storage resilience, relative to current operations? Third, does a variable-resolution FFMP structure with more storage zones improve performance or robustness relative to the current zone structure?

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 describes the Delaware River Basin test case and the Pywr-DRB simulation model. Section 3 details the methods, comprising the many-objective problem formulation, the three scenario designs and the hazard-filling selector, the experimental design, and the held-out re-evaluation protocol. Section 4 presents the results, Section 5 discusses their implications, and Section 6 concludes.

## 2. The Delaware River Basin Test Case

### 2.1 The NYC reservoir system and institutional water management

The Delaware River Basin (DRB) spans New York, Pennsylvania, New Jersey, and Delaware, and supplies approximately one half of New York City's drinking water through three upper-basin reservoirs (Cannonsville, Pepacton, and Neversink) whose diversions pass through the Rondout balancing reservoir and the Delaware Aqueduct. Basin operations are governed by the 1954 U.S. Supreme Court Decree in *New Jersey v. New York*, which caps NYC diversions at 800 million gallons per day (MGD), entitles New Jersey (NJ) to 100 MGD, and obligates NYC to make compensating releases that maintain a minimum flow of 1,750 cfs (1,131 MGD) at Montague, NJ. A companion flow objective of 3,000 cfs (1,939 MGD) at Trenton, NJ, protects downstream ecosystems and repels the salt front from the Philadelphia and Camden water intakes. Changes to the operation of NYC's Delaware reservoirs require unanimous consent of the five Decree parties.

Since 2007, releases have been managed under successive versions of the Flexible Flow Management Program, most recently the 2017 FFMP. The FFMP is a staged, storage-indexed rule structure. Combined NYC storage is compared daily against seasonally varying threshold curves that define operating zones ranging from flood-mitigation levels through normal operations to three drought stages. Each zone prescribes diversion curtailments for NYC and NJ and scaling factors on each reservoir's minimum release, modulated by seasonal release profiles, and additional flood-mitigation releases apply above the highest storage zones.

The central operational tension is structural. During severe drought, NYC's authorized diversion, the Montague compensating releases, and downstream ecological flows cannot all be met simultaneously, while wet-period storage management trades flood mitigation against drought reserve. This governance structure supplies a natural many-objective formulation. The FFMP's parameterized rules, which consist of negotiated quantities such as release baselines, drought curtailment factors, and storage-zone boundaries, supply a decision space whose re-optimization is institutionally meaningful because the rule structure is preserved and only its negotiated constants are varied (Section 3.1).

### 2.2 Pywr-DRB simulation of current operations

All policy evaluations use Pywr-DRB, an open-source daily water-availability and drought-risk model of the basin built on the Pywr simulation framework (Hamilton et al., 2024). Pywr-DRB represents the four NYC reservoirs and the major lower-basin reservoirs, the FFMP rule structure with its storage zones, seasonal release profiles, drought curtailments, and flood-mitigation releases, the Montague and Trenton flow targets with their time-of-travel lags, and NYC and NJ deliveries under the Decree's running-average accounting.

During optimization we use a trimmed configuration of the model in which the non-NYC reservoirs are simulated once under their default rules and their releases are supplied to the trimmed model as fixed boundary inflows. Each function evaluation therefore solves only the NYC-relevant portion of the network. This simplification is admissible because the decision variables (Section 3.1) alter only NYC operations, and the lower-basin reservoirs' rules do not respond to NYC decisions in the model. Because the boundary releases are policy-independent, they are computed once per realization and reused for every policy evaluated on that realization. The held-out re-evaluation (Section 3.4) therefore also runs the trimmed configuration, with a single presimulation pass over the re-evaluation ensemble. The full model is used only to generate the boundary releases and to simulate the status-quo baseline policy on the historical trace. Verification that the trimmed model reproduces full-model objective values under matched inputs is reported in Text S1 in Supporting Information S1.

## 3. Methods

### 3.1 Many-objective problem formulation

[FIGURE 2. Methods-overview schematic with lettered panels, showing (a) the end-to-end workflow from stochastic generation through candidate pool, scenario designs, many-objective search, and held-out re-evaluation; (b) the hazard-filling selection step; and (c) the re-evaluation and comparison step. Each Methods subsection references its panel. Figure file not yet produced.]

We pose the re-optimization of NYC's reservoir operations as the many-objective problem

$$
\min_{\mathbf{x} \in \mathcal{X}} \; \mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x}; E) = \big(J_1(\mathbf{x}; E), \ldots, J_8(\mathbf{x}; E)\big)
\quad \text{s.t.} \quad c_{\mathrm{del}}(\mathbf{x}) \le 0, \;\; c_{\mathrm{fld}}(\mathbf{x}) \le 0,
\tag{1}
$$

where $\mathbf{x}$ is the decision vector parameterizing the FFMP rule structure (Equation 2, detailed in Section 3.1.1), $E$ is the search ensemble of streamflow realizations presented to the search (Section 3.2), $J_1, \ldots, J_8$ are the objectives aggregated over $E$ (Section 3.1.2, Table 2; maximization objectives are negated so that all objectives are posed to the algorithm in minimization form), and $c_{\mathrm{del}}$ and $c_{\mathrm{fld}}$ are two feasibility constraints computed from decision-variable arithmetic alone, requiring monotone delivery curtailment across drought stages and ordered flood-zone releases (Section 3.1.1).

#### 3.1.1 Decision variables

The decision vector parameterizes the 2017 FFMP rule structure with 36 variables,

$$
\mathbf{x} = \big[\, \boldsymbol{\delta}^{\mathrm{NYC}}, \; \boldsymbol{\delta}^{\mathrm{NJ}}, \; \mathbf{v}, \; \boldsymbol{\tau}, \; \mathbf{s}^{\mathrm{rel}}, \; \mathbf{s}^{\mathrm{tgt}}, \; \mathbf{s}^{\mathrm{fld}} \,\big],
\tag{2}
$$

where $\boldsymbol{\delta}^{\mathrm{NYC}}$ holds NYC diversion curtailment factors for the three drought stages and $\boldsymbol{\delta}^{\mathrm{NJ}}$ holds NJ curtailment factors for the two deepest stages (5 variables), $\mathbf{v}$ holds nine vertical shifts of the storage-zone threshold curves, $\boldsymbol{\tau}$ holds six temporal shifts that slide each curve along the day-of-year axis, $\mathbf{s}^{\mathrm{rel}}$ holds four seasonal scaling factors on the daily minimum-release profiles of the conservation zones, $\mathbf{s}^{\mathrm{tgt}}$ holds six scaling factors on the Montague and Trenton drought-stage flow-target factor tables (one per gauge and drought stage), and $\mathbf{s}^{\mathrm{fld}}$ holds six scaling factors on the flood-zone spill-mitigation release schedules (one per zone and reservoir). Table 1 lists every variable with its baseline value and bounds.

The zone-boundary adjustment is restricted to plateau shifts and one temporal shift per curve. Each threshold curve is a trapezoid over the year whose low storage plateau, the fall and winter void, carries its own vertical shift. The three drought-stage curves, whose high plateaus sit below capacity, carry an independent shift of the high plateau, the spring and summer refill target, so void depth is decoupled from the refill target. The three flood-zone curves refill to full capacity and their high plateaus are held fixed, because the FFMP treats spring refill-to-full as an essential requirement and a lowered refill target would constitute a permanent forfeit of effective storage capacity rather than a renegotiation-scale adjustment of the operating rules. The FFMP's negotiated flood instrument, the seasonal void depth, remains fully searchable through the low-plateau shifts. This restriction keeps each adjustment legible to basin stakeholders, since every variable maps to a visible flat segment of a rule curve, and every curve retains its trapezoidal shape because the two ramps re-interpolate between the shifted plateaus.

The flood-release scales are season-invariant by design, matching the FFMP, which holds these rows constant across its tables and seasons. Seasonal flood policy instead enters through the zone-boundary shifts, mirroring the FFMP's own structure, in which seasonal flood mitigation is encoded in the storage-zone geometry rather than the release rates. The seasonal minimum-release scales use the FFMP's own season definitions (winter December through March, spring April and May, summer June through August, fall September through November); because the FFMP tables' finer date bins nest within these seasons, seasonally scaled schedules change value only on the FFMP's own bin boundaries.

Three quantity classes are held fixed rather than optimized. The 1954 Decree quantities (the Montague and Trenton flow targets and the NYC diversion cap) are legal anchors of the comparison. The reservoir minimum-release baselines are fixed because the FFMP itself never varies them; operational variation enters through the forecast-based table selection that the seasonal scaling factors emulate. The FFMP's maximum combined discharge rates are physical and regulatory limits on the release works and spillways at which scheduled flood-zone releases are capped.

Bounds are set from the FFMP's own structure and operational evidence, with provenance for each bound given in the note to Table 1. The NYC and NJ curtailment factors share one symmetric rule, in which each bound is the negotiated FFMP factor plus or minus 0.15, clipped at full delivery, so both Decree parties may be curtailed or relieved to the same depth around the negotiated compromise and every searched policy remains a renegotiation-scale perturbation of the FFMP. Feasibility is enforced at evaluation time by monotonicity clamps, so that storage-zone curves may not cross, deeper drought stages may not permit larger diversions, and flood-zone releases obey the zone ordering. The delivery-monotonicity and flood-ordering conditions are additionally posed to the optimizer as the formal constraints of Equation 1, computed from decision-variable arithmetic alone, so infeasible vectors are discarded under constraint-dominance before any simulation is spent on them. The zone-boundary conditions remain clamp-only because the clamped geometry is itself the intended policy. The FFMP's structural logic, including its storage-indexed stages, seasonal profiles, and running-average delivery accounting, is preserved exactly.

For the third research question, a variable-resolution generalization (denoted FFMP-*n*) interpolates the storage-zone structure to *n* zones and adds curtailment and shift variables per zone, sweeping *n* over the values 8, 10, and 12. Because the sweep multiplies search cost, it is scheduled against the compute reserve remaining at the end of the campaign (Section 3.3).

#### 3.1.2 Objectives and aggregation during search

The objective set spans the stakeholder priorities that the basin's governance encodes, comprising NYC supply, the Decree flow obligations protecting New Jersey and Philadelphia, downstream flood exposure, and reservoir-system resilience. Eight objectives are active (Table 2). The eighth, NJ delivery reliability, carries New Jersey's independent Decree interest; a redundancy screen over 512 constraint-feasible policies on each campaign search ensemble confirmed it is not collinear with any other objective (Spearman $|\rho| \le 0.38$ everywhere, and $\le 0.08$ with Trenton flow reliability; Text S5 in Supporting Information S1).

All metrics are computed on the metric window of each simulation, the daily output from six calendar months after its start, cut by date, and with weekly accounting following the Decree convention. Six months is the accumulation period the six-month Standardized Streamflow Index (SSI-6) requires before its first defined value, so the hazard metrics that select scenarios (Section 3.2.3) exclude the same interval and selection and evaluation score the identical window.

Compliance is always scored against static goalposts, namely the 1954 Decree quantities of 800 MGD for NYC, 1,131.05 MGD at Montague, and 100 MGD for NJ, together with the FFMP's Trenton equivalent-flow objective of 1,938.95 MGD, and never against the FFMP's live drought-adjusted targets. Scoring against the live target would allow a policy to satisfy its obligations by triggering the drought step-downs that lower those obligations. For NYC and NJ supply the Decree limits the running average of diversion rather than any single day's, so daily demand is not clipped at the static right. Instead each day's delivery target is the realizable entitlement, the smaller of that day's demand and the running-average allowance banked to it under the FFMP delivery accounting, reconstructed at the static right so that a policy's own drought step-downs cannot lower it. A demand spike above the flat daily right is honored whenever prior under-use has banked the allowance for it, while demand beyond the banked right is not counted as owed.

Two formulation choices depart from common practice deliberately, following the objective-stability findings of Quinn et al. (2017) and the convergence findings of Bonham et al. (2024). First, tail objectives use the conditional value-at-risk at the 90 percent level (CVaR90), defined as the mean of the worst decile of weekly deficits (Rockafellar and Uryasev, 2000; Löhndorf, 2016; Fairbrother et al., 2022), rather than the single worst week, which Quinn et al. (2017) identify as a high-variance, low-information signal. Second, flood exposure is measured as downstream flood severity, the mean annual sum over days of the largest exceedance, across three vulnerable reservoir-tail gauges (Hale Eddy, Fishs Eddy, and Bridgeville), of river stage above the National Weather Service minor flood stage, in units of ft-days per year and normalized per metric-window year so the metric is invariant to record length. This magnitude-weighted form resolves policy differences that a day count collapses onto a few integer values, tracks observed annual flood magnitude, and avoids the known pathologies of expected-damage objectives (Quinn et al., 2017); flood-day counts at the minor, major, and action stages are retained as reported diagnostics. Storage resilience uses the fifth percentile of aggregate NYC storage rather than the single-day minimum. Trenton flow reliability stands in for salt-front position because the Trenton target exists to repel the salt front, which makes the two physically redundant, and because the flow form avoids reliance on a statistical salinity emulator in the extreme droughts where objective values are decided.

For the single-trace Historical design (Section 3.2.1) the temporal metrics of Table 2 are the objectives. Each ensemble design must additionally collapse a matrix of objective values across realizations to one scalar per objective at each function evaluation. Following the aggregation formalism of Quinn et al. (2017) and Zatarain Salazar et al. (2017), each objective is expressed as the composition of a within-realization temporal aggregator $\Phi$ and an across-ensemble filter $\Psi$,

$$
J_d \;=\; \Psi_{(r,y)}\Big[\, \Phi_{t \in y}\big[\, g_d(t, r) \,\big] \,\Big],
\qquad r = 1, \ldots, N, \;\; y = 1, \ldots, L-1,
\tag{3}
$$

where $g_d(t, r)$ is the daily value underlying objective $d$ on day $t$ of realization $r$, $\Phi$ aggregates within each whole water-year unit $y$ of the metric window to produce an annual metric per realization-year, and $\Psi$ filters across the pooled $N(L-1)$ unit-years. Scenario windows are October-aligned, so the metric window opens on April 1 of the first water year and the first whole water-year unit is the second water year. The per-objective instantiations of $\Phi$ and $\Psi$ are listed in Table 2.

The reliability objectives take $\Phi$ as an annual failure indicator, which registers a failure when at least $k$ weeks in the unit-year violate the corresponding goalpost, and $\Psi$ as the frequency of non-failure years. This satisficing-frequency form is the search-time reliability operator used throughout the regional water-supply lineage (Zeff et al., 2014; Trindade et al., 2017; Gold et al., 2023). The failure-week counts were screened for saturation across both campaign ensemble compositions (Text S5 in Supporting Information S1); Trenton and NJ use $k = 1$ because larger counts saturate the Trenton ranking, and NYC delivery and Montague use $k = 3$. The deficit objectives take $\Phi$ as the within-year CVaR90 deficit and the storage objective as the annual minimum of daily aggregate storage; all three take $\Psi$ as the worst first-percentile unit-year, the stable tail form of Quinn et al. (2017, 2018). The flood-severity objective takes $\Psi$ as the mean across unit-years, corresponding to expected annual severity; a 99th-percentile variant was screened and retained as a diagnostic because at the campaign's pooled denominator it collapses onto a few values and is an order of magnitude noisier under bootstrap (Text S5 in Supporting Information S1).

The Historical record is scored as consecutive annual units with inherited state, following Quinn et al. (2018), so it carries the same unit denominator per realization-year as the ensemble designs (76 metric-bearing unit-years on its 77-year trace; unit boundaries are scoring windows, not restarts). Unit-years within a realization are serially dependent, since consecutive failure-years are precisely how multi-year droughts express themselves in this scheme, so the effective sample size is below $N(L-1)$. At the campaign size ($N = 100$, $L = 10$) the pooled denominator is 900 unit-years, comparable to the 1,000 one-year members that Quinn et al. (2017, 2018) use to stabilize the same worst-first-percentile operator, which is the ground for a per-evaluation ensemble smaller than the 1,000-realization convention of the regional water-supply lineage (Section 3.3).

Two properties of this scheme matter for the comparison. First, the temporal metrics of Table 2 and the entire held-out re-evaluation metric set (Section 3.5) are identical across designs. Only the across-realization stage differs, and it differs only because the two ensemble designs present different scenario compositions to it. Second, cross-design commensurability during search is neither required nor claimed. A design that deliberately over-represents severe hazard conditions computes search objectives under a distribution that differs from the generating process (Section 3.2.5), and the held-out re-evaluation is the only comparison point.

### 3.2 Scenario designs for the search ensemble

#### 3.2.1 The three designs

We compare three constructions of the search ensemble, all drawn from a single stationary population and all evaluated at matched size and record length except where a design cannot be matched by construction (Table 3). A survey of search-ensemble constructions in the water-resources policy-search literature is provided in Text S11 in Supporting Information S1.

The first design, the Historical design (HIST), is the observed record simulated as one continuous trace, with one realization of length 77 years. It is the reference for prevailing applied practice (Giuliani et al., 2016; Herman et al., 2020). It cannot be matched in ensemble size or budget and is reported as a reference rather than entered into the controlled contrast.

The second design, the Probabilistic Sampling design (PS), consists of $N$ realizations of length $L$ drawn i.i.d. from the stationary stochastic generator and frozen for the entire search. This is the standard construction of the regional water-supply lineage (Quinn et al., 2017; Zatarain Salazar et al., 2017) and the reference against which designed selection is judged. The contrast between it and the third design is the core of the first research question.

The third design, the Hazard-Filling design (HF), is the proposed method. It selects $N$ realizations from a large candidate pool by space-filling coverage of the hydrologic hazard space, using the selector of Section 3.2.4. It is constructed from the same stationary generator as PS, at the same $N$ and $L$, so the two designs differ only in the rule that selects which realizations enter the search ensemble.

The stationary population is the flow model of the prevailing water-supply optimization lineage (Herman et al., 2014; Trindade et al., 2017; Zatarain Salazar et al., 2017; Gold et al., 2023). Hazard variation within it arises entirely from natural variability, in that some 10-year windows contain severe multi-year droughts and most do not. Restricting the search to this population is deliberate. It isolates the first research question, which is whether the rule that selects realizations from a fixed generating process matters, without confounding that rule with a change in the generating process itself. The deeply uncertain climate-forcing space enters the study only in the held-out re-evaluation ensemble (Section 3.4), where it makes the re-evaluation a test of generalization to conditions absent from search. Prior work supports hazard-oriented selection within a stationary population: Zatarain Salazar et al. (2017) stratify a stationary pool by a realized-flow statistic, and Herman et al. (2016) amplify drought severity with a stationary weighted bootstrap.

#### 3.2.2 The generator and the candidate pool

Daily multi-site realizations are generated by the Kirsch-Nowak pipeline. Cholesky-based monthly generation preserves cross-site and lag correlations (Kirsch et al., 2013), and a nearest-neighbour resampling scheme disaggregates monthly flows to daily flows (Nowak et al., 2010). The generator is fitted to the reconstructed observed record (1945-2022) and run without climate perturbation. Generation mechanics, the deterministic seed architecture, and the statistical validation of generated flows are documented in Text S2 in Supporting Information S1.

Both ensemble designs draw from this one generator, and the distinction between them is the mechanism of selection. A design can generate as many independent realizations as it wants, but it cannot instruct the generator to produce a realization at a prescribed drought severity, because hazard coordinates are emergent properties of a realized sequence, computed only after the sequence exists. A design that targets hazard coverage must therefore select realizations from a finite pool of already-generated sequences rather than generate directly to a target, and the nearest-neighbour step in the selector (Section 3.2.4) is a consequence of this fact. A generator purpose-built to emit sequences at prescribed hazard targets is an alternative construction (Zaniolo et al., 2023, 2024), which we do not pursue because it forgoes the simulation-free scalability of selecting from a pool and requires committing to a small number of discrete target types.

The HF design owns a candidate pool of $P = 10^6$ realizations sampled independently from the stationary generator. The pool size is set by a nested pool-size diagnostic, since tail resolution in the selection space improves with $P$ (Section 3.2.4), and is inexpensive to enlarge: generating the pool involves no system simulation, and a $10^6$-member pool costs roughly 600 core-hours, under two percent of the campaign allocation across its replicated re-rolls. The pool is never simulated. Only each realization's hazard coordinates and generation seed are stored, and the $N$ selected members are regenerated exactly on demand from a deterministic, globally indexed random-stream architecture (Text S2 in Supporting Information S1). This storage arrangement is what makes million-member pools tractable.

The requirement that the candidate pool be sampled independently, rather than by a structured design such as a Latin hypercube, is essential to the validity of the comparison. A uniform random subset of size $N$ taken from an i.i.d. pool has exactly the joint distribution of $N$ fresh independent draws. This distributional equivalence is what makes PS the exact statistical control for HF: the two designs then differ only in the rule that selects $N$ members from the same population law, and any difference in re-evaluated robustness is attributable to that rule alone. The condition is enforced programmatically in the study workflow.

#### 3.2.3 Realizations, windows, and hazard metrics

Every ensemble scenario is a disjoint, non-overlapping $L$-year realization. Each simulation starts from a fixed initial storage of 80 percent of capacity, which constitutes a terminating-simulation design, and the first six months of each window are excluded from all metrics (Section 3.1.2). A drought event that would straddle a window boundary is split by it; the choice of $L$ bounds this truncation (Section 3.3), and event-scale severity is additionally captured at re-evaluation, where records are longer.

Hazard coordinates are computed on the aggregate inflow to the three NYC reservoirs before any system simulation. The candidate axes are drought-event metrics derived from run theory applied to the SSI-6, consisting of the deficit volume, duration, peak depth, onset rate, and recovery rate of the controlling event (McKee et al., 1993; Vicente-Serrano et al., 2012), together with flood metrics from a peaks-over-threshold analysis of daily flows, consisting of the peak magnitude, pulse duration, and rise rate. The event models are fixed as follows. A drought event is a run of SSI-6 below zero that reaches minus one or lower, terminated by three consecutive non-negative months. When a window contains more than one event, the controlling event, the one with the largest cumulative deficit and the operationally binding event under fixed initial storage, is scored, and all five dry descriptors are zero for a window with no qualifying event. On the wet side, the high-flow threshold is the 95th percentile of the observed record's daily flow, and the critical pulse is the exceedance run containing the window's maximum daily flow, so exactly one pulse is scored per window and no declustering parameter is required. Peak magnitude is the window maximum normalized by the observed mean daily flow, pulse duration is the number of days above threshold within that run, and rise rate is the largest one-day rise on the pulse's rising limb, likewise normalized. The SSI distribution fit, the flood threshold, and the normalizing mean are all fixed once on the observed record and reused for every realization, so hazard coordinates are comparable across the pool and the Historical reference. Full descriptor definitions are given in Text S3 in Supporting Information S1.

Two scope properties of this hazard space are acknowledged. First, the axes are defined on aggregate NYC inflow only. The flows deciding the Montague, Trenton, and flood-gauge objectives include unregulated tributary contributions the hazard image never sees, and a supporting check correlates selected-ensemble hazard coordinates against realized stress at the downstream objective sites (Text S3 in Supporting Information S1). Second, the descriptors characterize a single controlling event per window, so no frequency, timing, or inter-event descriptor survives to the selection, and a window with two moderate droughts and a window with one are indistinguishable in this coordinate system. Event multiplicity is left as an explicit extension.

The descriptor set is screened on the pool's hazard image before selection. Degenerate descriptors with near-zero spread are dropped, and near-duplicate pairs, with absolute Spearman rank correlation of at least 0.95, are pruned to a single canonical member so that one hazard concept cannot enter the selection distance twice under two names. On this population the screen retains all eight candidate descriptors. The largest pairwise rank correlation, 0.88 between deficit volume and duration, is below the pruning cut, is stable across pool sizes from $2 \times 10^3$ to $10^6$ members, and the retained set is insensitive to tightening the cut to 0.90. Six of the retained descriptors serve as the selection axes, namely deficit volume, peak depth, onset rate, and recovery rate on the drought side, and peak magnitude and pulse duration on the flood side. The two remaining descriptors are excluded from the selection distance: drought duration is the most redundant member of the retained set (rank correlation 0.87 with deficit volume, with a quasi-discrete, months-valued tail), and flood rise rate is the flood descriptor most entangled with the retained pair (rank correlation 0.60 with peak magnitude). Limiting the selection space matters because the selector's reach into the severe corners weakens as axes are added at fixed pool size (Section 3.2.4). Both excluded descriptors are still computed for every pool member and reported as post-hoc diagnostics, and the redundancy structure of the descriptor set, characterized following the index-redundancy logic of Olden and Poff (2003), is reported in Text S3 in Supporting Information S1, together with a selection-invariance check that quantifies the weighting that correlated axes induce in the selection distance.

#### 3.2.4 The hazard-filling selector

The selector fills the selection space, the six hazard axes of Section 3.2.3, in absolute, range-scaled magnitude units. Each selection axis is scaled to the unit interval by its robust pool range, the per-axis 1st and 99th percentiles, with members beyond them clipped to the box faces, so that no single axis dominates distances while the spacing within each axis remains proportional to physical magnitude. Robust percentile bounds, rather than the sample extremes, make the selection geometry a stable functional of the population. The sample minimum and maximum of a right-skewed hazard metric are non-convergent extreme order statistics, so a full-range box would grow with the pool size $P$, vary materially across the pool re-rolls, and allow a single outlier to compress the bulk of the pool into a corner of the box. The clipped mass is bounded at one percent per axis tail and is reported, with the realized bounds, as a build diagnostic. A bounds sweep confirms the choice empirically, with full-range scaling degrading realized coverage roughly threefold through outlier fixation while coverage and enrichment vary smoothly across nearby robust-percentile settings (Text S4 in Supporting Information S1).

$N$ Latin hypercube anchors are drawn in the scaled hazard box, and each anchor is snapped to the nearest not-yet-selected pool member in Euclidean distance. The selector is deterministic given its anchor seed, involves no iterative optimization of a coverage criterion, and has no tuning parameters. Because Latin hypercube anchors stratify every axis marginally regardless of dimension, the selector's guarantee is stratified marginal coverage with tail enrichment on each selection axis. Joint coverage is reported descriptively against a random design of the same size and dimension (Text S4 in Supporting Information S1) rather than claimed as uniform filling.

The strength of the enrichment the selector achieves is set jointly by the pool size and the dimension of the selection space. The expected distance from an anchor to its nearest pool member scales as $P^{-1/m}$ on $m$ axes, so at fixed pool size each added axis dilutes the snap and pulls selected members back toward the bulk of the pool. In the coverage diagnostics, mean snap distance roughly triples between four-axis and eight-axis selection on the same pool, with per-axis tail enrichment falling accordingly. The design responds on both margins. The selection space is held to the six axes of Section 3.2.3 rather than the full descriptor set, and the pool size is set at $P = 10^6$ from a nested pool-size diagnostic in which the selection is repeated on nested prefixes of a single large pool, which are honest i.i.d. pools of every intermediate size under the global-index seed architecture (Text S2 in Supporting Information S1). At the production configuration, every selection axis places roughly three times the unbiased share of selected members in its upper decile (at least 31 percent of selected members above the pool's 90th percentile, against the 10 percent that i.i.d. sampling delivers), and this realized enrichment is re-measured on every production draw as a build diagnostic (Text S4 in Supporting Information S1).

Absolute-magnitude scaling is the operative choice, and it is what makes the design a genuine intervention rather than a variance-reduction adjustment. Because the hazard marginals of a stochastic generator are strongly right-skewed, most pool members cluster near the center of the hazard space and few occupy the severe corners. Filling the range uniformly therefore draws selected members from the sparse corners far more often than their pool frequency, so the severe drought and flood conditions that decide reservoir performance are over-represented in the search ensemble relative to their probability under the generator. This is the deliberate distribution shift that the first research question tests. An alternative that transforms each axis by its empirical cumulative distribution before filling would instead reproduce the pool's marginal frequencies and distort only the joint dependence among axes. That rank-space variant is registered as a non-campaign sensitivity because it isolates how much of any effect is attributable to tail over-representation specifically.

The rationale for uniform coverage of a condition space, as opposed to probability-faithful sampling of it, descends from the bottom-up and decision-scaling traditions, which sample condition spaces uniformly to map system response rather than to estimate expectations (Brown et al., 2012; Culley et al., 2016; Herman et al., 2016; Fowler et al., 2024). We take from that tradition the coverage argument for constructing the ensemble, not a precedent for biasing a reported robustness number. The selector is deliberately the simplest space-filling design so that its construction variance is measurable under replication (Section 3.3) and so that coverage statistics remain an independent verification of what the selector achieved, reported as a method diagnostic rather than as a comparison result (Text S4 in Supporting Information S1).

#### 3.2.5 The consequence of the distribution shift

The two ensemble designs present different scenario distributions to the search. PS presents the generator's own distribution, and HF presents a distribution deliberately shifted toward severe hazard conditions. Any objective aggregated over the search ensemble is therefore computed under a different measure for the two designs, and for HF that measure over-weights the conditions in which policies are hardest to satisfy. The shift biases which policies the optimizer selects, not merely a reported number, which is why it is a design intervention worth testing and why search objectives are never compared across designs.

The held-out re-evaluation removes the evaluation bias of scoring each design on its own ensemble, because both designs are scored on the same instrument. It does not remove, and is not intended to remove, the selection effect that the hazard-filling construction embodies. Whether over-representing severe hazard conditions during search yields policies that are more robust under held-out deeply uncertain re-evaluation, or merely tailors policies to conditions that re-evaluation does not reward, is the empirical question, and the common re-evaluation ensemble is what makes the two outcomes distinguishable. Importance-sampling reweighting of the search objectives back to the generator's measure is not attempted. The snapped-selection rule induces no tractable density, so exact weights are not available, and even an approximate reweighting would defeat the purpose of the intervention, which is to let severe conditions drive the search.

### 3.3 Experimental design

#### 3.3.1 Budget control and sizing

Both matched designs use $N = 100$ realizations of $L = 10$ years, which amounts to 1,000 scenario-years per function evaluation, and both run at equal number of function evaluations (NFE). Because $N$ and $L$ are common, the per-evaluation simulation cost, scenario-years, and wall-clock time are identical across designs, so equal NFE and equal scenario-years coincide, giving one budget condition and no confound between ensemble composition and search effort.

The common $N$ and $L$ are required rather than convenient. If $L$ differed across designs, the selection rule would be confounded with record length, and at a fixed per-evaluation budget $N$ and $L$ trade off directly. The value of $N$ is bounded below by the coverage requirement. The Latin hypercube anchors stratify each selection axis into $N$ bins, so marginal coverage and tail resolution sharpen with $N$, and the achieved per-axis and joint coverage are reported against a random design of the same size and dimension (Text S4 in Supporting Information S1). The coverage diagnostics support $N = 100$ directly. Every selection axis is better stratified and more tail-enriched than a random design of the same size, and enlarging $N$ at fixed pool size lowers per-axis tail enrichment rather than raising it, because the pool holds only $P/10$ members above the 90th percentile of any axis and additional selections increasingly draw from the bulk. A long-record design, for example $L = 50$ years forcing $N$ to approximately 20, would leave too few anchors for meaningful stratification. The value $L = 10$ years in turn exceeds the duration of the DRB drought of record in the 1960s, which lasted four to five years, plus its onset and recovery, so a design-basis event fits within a single window and duration-type hazard axes are not truncation-limited. The per-evaluation ensemble of 100 realizations is smaller than the 1,000-realization convention of the regional water-supply lineage; the ground for it is the annual-unit pooling of Equation 3, which yields 900 metric-bearing unit-years per evaluation, comparable to the sample on which that lineage stabilizes the same tail operators. The sampling variance of the worst-first-percentile operator at $N = 100$ is quantified by the epsilon-calibration bootstrap (Text S5 in Supporting Information S1). The HIST design, with one realization of about 77 years, cannot be budget-matched and is reported as a reference throughout.

The function-evaluation budget is 500,000 NFE per search, within the range used for comparable reservoir control-policy problems in this lineage (Quinn et al., 2017; Bartholomew and Kwakkel, 2020). Because the runtime archive (Section 3.3.3) records intermediate NFE levels, the attained budget is justified against observed convergence behavior after the fact, and the design comparison is recomputable at earlier budgets at re-evaluation cost only.

#### 3.3.2 Replication

Two sources of variability are separated by design. A draw is a scenario design's entire construction re-run from scratch with a fresh seed. For PS a draw is a fresh i.i.d. sample; for HF a draw is a fresh candidate pool together with a fresh anchor plan. Re-drawing the pool within each draw is essential to the comparison, because generating the pool is part of the hazard-filling construction, and pinning it across draws would make HF appear more stable than PS as an artifact of the replication scheme rather than as a finding. A seed is an independent MOEA trial on a fixed draw. Each matched design runs $K = 3$ draws with $S = 2$ seeds per draw, set against the compute allocation. The HIST design has $K = 1$, because its composition variance is zero by construction, and is replicated across $S$ seeds only.

The unit of analysis for the between-design comparison is the draw, and seeds within a draw are treated as pseudoreplicates. Draw-level and seed-level results are reported transparently, so the design effect is read against the observed construction and search variability. This replication structure separates ensemble-construction variance from search stochasticity, which distinguishes the present comparison from its nearest antecedent, Eker and Kwakkel (2018), which compared a single designed scenario set against a single random set and could not separate a genuine design effect from the variance of a single draw.

#### 3.3.3 Optimization algorithm and computational experiment

All searches use the Multi-Master Borg MOEA (Hadka and Reed, 2013, 2015), whose epsilon-dominance archiving, adaptive operator selection, and randomized restarts have repeatedly proven reliable for many-objective water resources problems (Reed et al., 2013; Zatarain Salazar et al., 2016, 2017). Each combination of design, draw, and seed is an independent search on the Purdue Anvil system, run as four islands of 254 evaluator ranks (1,021 MPI ranks on eight nodes) to the 500,000-NFE budget, with the calibrated epsilon-dominance precisions of Table 2 shared by every search. Runtime archive snapshots are logged at a fixed NFE interval. Node-packing, per-evaluation cost, and strong-scaling measurements that size the campaign, together with the service-unit accounting, are reported in Text S8 in Supporting Information S1.

Within each design, search progress is monitored through the algorithm's runtime dynamics and reported per seed as a diagnostic, following the runtime-metrics protocol of Reed et al. (2013) and the epsilon-progress and hypervolume conventions of Hadka and Reed (2015) (Text S7 in Supporting Information S1). These internal quantities are never compared across designs, because objective values computed on different search ensembles are not commensurable and because reference-set metrics scored against a pooled frontier are biased in a design-dependent way (Section 3.5.4). Convergence assessment is not used as a stopping rule, so no design benefits from a tuned termination criterion, and both matched designs execute the identical NFE budget. Because the runtime archive records the approximation set at intermediate NFE levels, the final design comparison can be recomputed at earlier budgets at re-evaluation cost only, which tests whether the design ranking depends on the chosen budget.

### 3.4 Held-out re-evaluation

#### 3.4.1 The re-evaluation ensemble

Every design's final Pareto-approximate set is re-simulated on a single common re-evaluation ensemble, using the trimmed model configuration of Section 2.2 with the policy-independent boundary releases presimulated once per realization, and all cross-design comparison is made there. The re-evaluation ensemble is never used during any search, is never the source of any search ensemble, and is generated from a reserved seed domain disjoint from that of the candidate pool. A collision between the search-side and test-side seed domains is treated as a hard error, which guards against the failure mode of testing on data the search has seen (Bonham et al., 2024).

The re-evaluation ensemble is the largest ensemble in the study by a wide margin, and it is built to encompass a deeply uncertain climate-forcing envelope rather than to represent a probability distribution. The forcing is a monthly change factor applied to the generator's fitted moments, parameterized as a low-order harmonic in the log change factor whose amplitudes govern annual volume, seasonal amplitude, and the snowmelt-shoulder shape, with bounds taken from a CMIP6 multimodel ensemble (Text S6 in Supporting Information S1). The adjustment preserves each month's coefficient of variation, so the forcing perturbs the mean seasonal profile without independently altering relative variability. The ensemble is a Latin hypercube over the full empirical range of the CMIP6-fitted amplitudes, widened by a 25 percent margin, an envelope deliberately wider than any variation the search ensembles contain, crossed with stochastic realizations per Latin hypercube point. Each Latin hypercube point is a SOW, and its realizations sample natural variability within that state. The ensemble comprises 1,000 SOWs, 25 realizations per SOW, and 50-year records, for 25,000 realizations and 1.25 million scenario-years. The SOW count fixes the Monte Carlo resolution of any reported satisficing fraction at plus or minus 1.6 percentage points in the worst case. Each SOW's 1,225 metric-bearing annual units match the sample at which this lineage's tail operators stabilize (Section 3.3.1), so the within-SOW performance estimate is resolved at least as well as one search evaluation. The 50-year records, five times the search-record length, additionally test sustained operation. Consecutive droughts are experienced on carried-over storage and the running-average delivery accounting matures, neither of which a terminating 10-year window can express. Where the re-evaluation ensemble itself is placed in the hazard space, its hazard coordinates are computed on disjoint, October-aligned 10-year sub-windows, so they are commensurable with the pool's window convention. Adequacy of both sample sizes is verified after re-evaluation by SOW-subsample and realization-subsample stability of the design rankings, scored offline from the persisted matrix (Section 3.4.2) at no additional simulation cost. This construction, a Latin hypercube of SOWs crossed with stochastic realizations, is the standard of the re-evaluation lineage (Kasprzyk et al., 2013; Trindade et al., 2017; Bartholomew and Kwakkel, 2020; Gold et al., 2022; Quinn et al., 2020).

Two properties of the re-evaluation ensemble carry the interpretation of the comparison. First, the search ensembles are drawn from the unperturbed stationary generator, whereas the re-evaluation ensemble spans a forced climate envelope that no search ensemble contains. The re-evaluation therefore measures whether hazard-space coverage of the natural-variability manifold produces policies that generalize to conditions absent from search, which is a stronger test than re-evaluating on the search distribution and which keeps the test instrument structurally distinct from both search designs. Neither matched design searches on a distribution resembling the re-evaluation ensemble, so the instrument does not structurally favor either design. Second, no robustness number reported in this study is an expectation. Under deep uncertainty there is no probability measure over the forcing space (Lamontagne et al., 2018). A satisficing fraction computed over the re-evaluation ensemble is a coverage-weighted count over a designed exploration of the deeply uncertain box, and it is reported as such. The cross-design comparison is commensurable because the re-evaluation ensemble is identical across designs, not because it is probability-faithful.

Following Quinn et al. (2020), we do not claim that the re-evaluation ensemble is scenario-neutral, because no experimental design is, and all design rankings are conditional on this one reference ensemble. The persisted re-evaluation matrix (Section 3.4.2) supports composition-sensitivity checks at no simulation cost, in which hazard-restricted and envelope-restricted subsets of the ensemble are re-scored to probe whether the ranking depends on the region of the test space emphasized (Text S10 in Supporting Information S1). The construction inherits the historical record's interannual wet-dry persistence, so persistence is stressed in neither the search population nor the re-evaluation ensemble, and claims are scoped to hold under historical-level interannual persistence (Text S2 in Supporting Information S1); NYC and NJ demands are likewise held at the constant Decree maxima, so no demand-growth axis enters either search or test.

#### 3.4.2 The persisted re-evaluation matrix

Re-evaluation persists the complete array of objective values, indexed by solution, realization, and objective, in natural units, together with each realization's SOW identifier. Every robustness metric in Section 3.5 is then an offline re-scoring of this array, and no metric choice, threshold change, or new metric requires re-simulation. In the taxonomy of McPhail et al. (2018), the persisted array is the substrate on which any performance transformation, scenario subset, and aggregation can be recomposed after the fact. Both natural units of robustness are thereby available. The realization unit applies the criterion across all 25,000 realizations. The SOW unit of the regional-supply lineage collapses each forcing point's realizations first, by a stated within-SOW aggregator, and then applies the criterion across the 1,000 SOWs (Herman et al., 2014; Trindade et al., 2017; Gold et al., 2022, 2023). Precision is governed by the SOW count rather than by the raw realization count, because realizations within a SOW are not independent, so uncertainty on ensemble-level quantities is assessed at the SOW level.

### 3.5 Comparison metrics

#### 3.5.1 The primary endpoint

The comparison endpoint for the first research question is the re-evaluated multivariate satisficing fraction of the policies a design produces, under Starr's domain criterion. For a single policy, the satisficing fraction on the re-evaluation ensemble is

$$
S(\mathbf{x}) \;=\; \frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{re}}} \sum_{j=1}^{N_{\mathrm{re}}} \Lambda_j(\mathbf{x}),
\qquad
\Lambda_j(\mathbf{x}) \;=\; \prod_{i} \mathbf{1}\!\left[\, f_{i}(\mathbf{x}, s_j) \;\text{meets}\; c_i \,\right],
\tag{4}
$$

where $N_{\mathrm{re}}$ is the number of re-evaluation realizations, $f_i(\mathbf{x}, s_j)$ is policy $\mathbf{x}$'s whole-trace temporal metric (Table 2) for objective $i$ on realization $s_j$, and $c_i$ is that objective's criterion. This is Starr's (1962) domain criterion, the standard robustness measure of the regional water-supply lineage (Herman et al., 2014, 2015; Trindade et al., 2017; Gold et al., 2022, 2023), and the fastest-converging robustness family in ensemble-size testing, with accurate rankings from 50 to 300 scenarios (Bonham et al., 2024).

A single search yields a Pareto-approximate set rather than one policy, so a run-level scalar is required before the between-design comparison of Section 3.3.2 can operate. The pre-specified run-level endpoint is the maximum satisficing fraction attained by any policy in the run's re-evaluated set, which answers the practical question of whether a design can find a robust policy at all. A maximum over a set inherits a mild upward bias that grows with set cardinality, so the per-objective satisficing decomposition is reported alongside and a single scalar does not carry the entire comparison.

The criteria $c_i$ are anchored on Decree and FFMP goalposts wherever one exists and are otherwise conventions, which is why they are swept (Section 3.5.3) rather than fixed at a single point. [Open item: the criterion anchor values.]

#### 3.5.2 Secondary metrics

Four secondary metrics are reported alongside the primary endpoint, all as offline re-scorings of the persisted matrix. Univariate satisficing gives the per-objective decomposition of the primary. The coverage-weighted mean across realizations is the risk-neutral reference, and the worst realization is the risk-averse reference; both are reported because metric choice is known to change robustness rankings (Herman et al., 2015; Giuliani and Castelletti, 2016; McPhail et al., 2018). The signed improvement over the status quo is the orientation-signed difference between each policy and the default 2017 FFMP policy simulated on the same re-evaluation ensemble, which answers the second research question by measuring whether re-optimization improves on current operations, and which serves as a fixed, design-independent reference in the deviation-from-baseline tradition of Kasprzyk et al. (2013). This last metric is a fixed-reference regret in the transformation taxonomy of McPhail et al. (2018); it does not move when designs are added or dropped, the property that a set-relative regret lacks. Agreement among the design rankings induced by these metrics is summarized with Kendall's rank correlation $\tau_b$ (McPhail et al., 2020), and because a robustness scalar can be stable, optimizable, and still misleading (Huang et al., 2025), the raw performance distribution on the re-evaluation ensemble, summarized by its median and spread, is co-reported alongside every robustness value.

No set-relative regret measure is computed. Best-in-set regret defines the reference by the best-performing policy in each scenario, so removing one design from the pooled set changes every other design's score, and it is the slowest-converging metric family in the ensemble-size testing of Bonham et al. (2024). Baseline regret in the sense of Cohen et al. (2021) requires one perfect-foresight optimization per scenario and does not scale to million-member candidate pools; we cite it as motivation and never compute it. No search-minus-test overfitting gap is reported, because under the HF design the in-sample term is computed under a deliberately shifted distribution, so the gap would grow with exactly the coverage the method advocates.

From the persisted array we additionally flag, at no simulation cost, the re-evaluation realizations in which no policy from either design meets the criteria. This attainability screen separates poor search performance from scenarios that are unwinnable for the evaluated policy pool; Shavazipour et al. (2021) found that 23 percent of their test scenarios were unwinnable by any policy. Each design's satisficing fraction is bounded above by the attainable fraction, which sets the ceiling against which the design difference is read. The screen provides an empirical bound over the evaluated policies rather than a per-scenario oracle, since an oracle would require exactly the perfect-foresight optimization that this study excludes.

#### 3.5.3 The criterion sweep

Where a satisficing criterion is not fixed by a Decree or FFMP goalpost, it descends from stakeholder elicitations (Zeff et al., 2014) and is a convention. We therefore anchor each criterion on a goalpost where one exists and sweep the remainder over a grid, asking not whether robustness values move, which they will, but whether the design difference is invariant. The sweep is necessary rather than cosmetic, because Quinn et al. (2020) show that robustness-rank agreement across experimental designs degrades as the satisficing criterion becomes more stringent, so a single fixed threshold could manufacture or hide the entire design effect. The sweep is implemented on a single stringency axis. At stringency $s$, each objective's criterion is placed at the oriented $s$-quantile of that objective's distribution pooled across all designs' re-evaluated policies, so that one value of $s$ excludes the same marginal fraction of pooled outcomes for every objective and every design, and $s$ is swept from lax to stringent with the Decree-anchored and FFMP-anchored criteria located on the same axis. Pooling across designs is what makes equal stringency mean equal severity for every design. Threshold-margin diagnostics, consisting of each objective's cumulative distribution across the re-evaluation ensemble with the criterion overlaid, follow Gold et al. (2023) and accompany the sweep (Text S10 in Supporting Information S1). The comparison is thereby reported as a difference that holds, or fails to hold, across the range of defensible criteria rather than as a single number at one threshold.

#### 3.5.4 Reference sets

Where dominance-based summaries over the pooled re-evaluated policies are reported, they are read with three caveats stated up front. A design contributes points to the frontier against which it is scored, the designs return different numbers of solutions (an asymmetry that Bartholomew and Kwakkel (2020) acknowledge but do not correct), and noisier re-evaluated estimates contribute more spuriously nondominated points (Shavazipour et al., 2021). Within-design MOEA search metrics, including hypervolume, are computed against per-design reference sets only and read purely as convergence diagnostics, following Zatarain Salazar et al. (2017), who established that reference sets must be built per search-ensemble level and that cross-level comparison is defensible only on a common independent verification ensemble. These summaries are supplementary (Text S9 in Supporting Information S1); the primary comparison is the endpoint of Section 3.5.1.

## 1. Introduction

Water supply systems worldwide face growing stress from hydroclimatic change. Many of their operating rules were negotiated decades ago under the assumption of a stationary hydrology and are increasingly required to perform under conditions outside their design basis. Many-objective evolutionary search coupled with simulation has become a standard tool for exploring how such rules could be adapted, and for revealing the tradeoffs among competing water uses that any renegotiation must confront (Reed et al., 2013; Giuliani et al., 2016; Herman et al., 2020). A defining feature of this approach is that every candidate policy is evaluated over a finite set of streamflow scenarios, thousands to millions of times during a single search. The composition of that scenario set determines which hydrologic conditions are represented during search, and therefore which vulnerabilities the resulting policies are shaped to manage. In most applications, however, the search ensemble is inherited rather than designed. The two most common choices are the historical record itself and an independent sample from a fitted stochastic generator.

The evidence that this choice matters is growing but two-sided. Cohen et al. (2021), the nearest antecedent to the present study, framed reservoir policy search as a training and testing problem and showed that the properties of the training scenarios determine out-of-sample robustness. In their experiment, policies trained on scenario subsets with high baseline regret matched the performance of policies trained on all scenarios at approximately one third of the computational cost, whereas subsets defined by coarse hydrologic aggregates failed to identify the valuable scenarios. Zaniolo et al. (2023) showed that the drought type embedded in a training ensemble changes not only performance but also the installed infrastructure, the technology mix, and the state variables that a policy consults. Brodeur et al. (2020) demonstrated that policies calibrated to a single hydrologic sequence overfit to its event sequencing and that resampled training sets generalize better. McPhail et al. (2020), comparing robustness assessments across scenario ensembles, found that changing the scenario set moves robustness *values* far more than robustness *rankings* — a null-direction precedent for any comparison whose endpoint is relative performance. The strongest available null result nevertheless cautions against assuming that any designed selection outperforms sampling. Eker and Kwakkel (2018) found that scenarios selected for policy relevance and diversity "[do] not lead to significantly more favorable results in terms of robustness or variety of robustness tradeoffs, compared to an arbitrary set of scenarios." In addition, Giuliani and Castelletti (2016) showed that policies designed under one across-scenario aggregation and scored under another are systematically dominated by policies designed under the scoring measure, which is a direct warning to any method that deliberately alters the scenario distribution during search. Whether a designed search ensemble can outperform a probabilistic one is therefore a genuinely open question rather than a foregone conclusion, and answering it requires an experiment built to detect the effect if it exists and to report its absence if it does not.

We approach that question by distinguishing three spaces that the literature often conflates. The input space contains the factors that define scenario generation, such as generator parameters, climate multipliers, and demand factors. A sampled point in this space is a state of the world (SOW) in the terminology of the robust-decision-making lineage (Kasprzyk et al., 2013; Trindade et al., 2017). The outcome space contains simulation outputs, which are the objective values of a candidate policy under a scenario. Between them sits what we term the hazard space, defined as the space of hydrologic hazard metrics computed directly on each realized streamflow sequence before any system simulation. Examples of hazard coordinates include drought event severity, duration, and intensity, together with low-flow and high-flow indices. Hazard coordinates are properties of the scenario rather than of the simulated system response, and they are computable for millions of candidate sequences at negligible cost. The closest existing construct is the exposure space of the scenario-neutral tradition (Culley et al., 2016; Fowler et al., 2024). Exposure spaces, however, are typically attribute targets imposed on generator inputs, whereas hazard coordinates are measured on realized output sequences.

The motivating observation is that neither of the prevailing search ensembles is designed to represent the hydrologic conditions that stress a system. The historical record contains one realization of natural variability, so its hazard content is whatever the observed decades happened to deliver. An independent sample from a stochastic generator reproduces the frequency of hazard conditions but, precisely because it is probability-faithful, it populates the common center of the hazard distribution densely and the severe corners sparsely, even though the severe corners are where reservoir policies are decided. Prior work has controlled hydrologic hazard in a search ensemble only in restricted ways. Zatarain Salazar et al. (2017) stratified a stationary synthetic pool on a single realized-flow statistic during search, in one dimension and preserving the parent probabilities. Herman et al. (2016) and Zaniolo et al. (2023, 2024) control drought properties at generation time, at a small number of discrete, analyst-chosen levels. Bonham et al. (2024) introduced space-filling subsampling of state-of-the-world ensembles to water resources, but for post-hoc robustness ranking rather than for the search ensemble. Beyond hydrology, scenario-subset selection for optimization has been outcome-informed: Giudici et al. (2020) use active learning to select the smallest informative scenario subset during robust optimization of an energy system, and Hilbers et al. (2019) subsample planning periods by an importance weight derived from simulated operating cost, deliberately over-representing the stressful tail — both approaches require simulating every candidate scenario through the system model, which is the dependence that selection in hazard space removes. Within the robustness-in-search line, Watson and Kasprzyk (2017) showed that the small scenario ensembles carried through many-objective search materially change the solutions found, and Carlsen et al. (2016) select small policy-relevant scenario sets by combining vulnerability and diversity criteria, though outside the search loop. Bartholomew and Kwakkel (2020) close their comparison of robustness-in-search methods with an open problem that this study takes up directly: "Research is needed into the selection [of] scenarios which as a set contain the appropriately stressing conditions against which solutions have to be robust, while also capturing the scenarios under which one would like to have near optimal performance."

This study contributes hazard filling, a search-ensemble construction that selects scenarios from a large candidate pool of synthetic streamflow sequences so that the retained members cover the hydrologic hazard space, deliberately over-representing the severe corners relative to their frequency under the generating process. The selection is simulation-free, because hazard coordinates are computed on the sequences alone, so the design scales to candidate pools of order one million sequences and transfers across objective formulations. Problem-driven selection properties such as baseline regret, by contrast, require one perfect-foresight optimization per candidate scenario (Cohen et al., 2021). The selector is deliberately the simplest defensible space-filling design, consisting of Latin hypercube anchors in the hazard space that are each snapped to the nearest unused pool member. We test this construction against the discipline's default, an independent and identically distributed sample from the same stochastic generator, in a controlled comparison at matched ensemble size, record length, and function-evaluation budget, on a real multi-reservoir system. The application is the re-optimization of New York City's Delaware River Basin reservoir operations within the Flexible Flow Management Program (FFMP) rule structure, simulated with the open-source Pywr-DRB model (Hamilton et al., 2024).

Because a design that deliberately alters the scenario distribution is not commensurable with a probabilistic one on its own search objectives, all cross-design comparison is made in a single way. Every design's Pareto-approximate policies are re-evaluated on one common, held-out test ensemble, and compared there with a fixed set of robustness metrics (Zatarain Salazar et al., 2017; McPhail et al., 2020). The test ensemble is constructed to be deeply uncertain: a Latin hypercube over a wide range of climate-forcing perturbations, crossed with stochastic realizations. Because the search ensembles are drawn from the unperturbed generator while the test ensemble spans a forced climate envelope, the comparison measures whether hazard-space coverage of the natural-variability manifold produces policies that generalize to conditions never presented during search. This is a stronger test than re-evaluating on the same distribution used to search, and it keeps the test instrument structurally distinct from both search ensembles.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to test hazard-space scenario selection against probabilistic sampling at matched ensemble size, sequence length, and search budget on a real system, with both designs re-evaluated on a common held-out, deeply uncertain ensemble and with a replication scheme that separates ensemble-construction variance from search variance. Three research questions structure the analysis.

1. Does constructing the search ensemble by hazard-space coverage, rather than by independent sampling from the same stochastic generator, change the robustness of the resulting Pareto-approximate policies under held-out, deeply uncertain re-evaluation? (RQ1, the core question.)
2. Can re-optimizing the parameters of the existing FFMP rule structure improve NYC and basin-wide outcomes, including water supply reliability, Montague and Trenton flow-target compliance, downstream flood exposure, and storage resilience, relative to current operations? (RQ2.)
3. Does a variable-resolution FFMP structure with more storage zones improve performance or robustness relative to the current zone structure? (RQ3.)

## 2. The Delaware River Basin Test Case

### 2.1 The NYC reservoir system and the Flexible Flow Management Program

The Delaware River Basin (DRB) spans New York, Pennsylvania, New Jersey, and Delaware, and supplies approximately one half of New York City's drinking water through three upper-basin reservoirs (Cannonsville, Pepacton, and Neversink) whose diversions pass through the Rondout balancing reservoir and the Delaware Aqueduct. Basin operations are governed by the 1954 U.S. Supreme Court Decree in *New Jersey v. New York*, which caps NYC diversions at 800 MGD, entitles New Jersey to 100 MGD, and obligates NYC to make compensating releases that maintain a minimum flow of 1,750 cfs (1,131 MGD) at Montague, NJ. A companion flow objective of 3,000 cfs (1,939 MGD) at Trenton, NJ, protects downstream ecosystems and repels the salt front from the Philadelphia and Camden water intakes. Changes to the operation of NYC's Delaware reservoirs require unanimous consent of the five Decree parties.

Since 2007, releases have been managed under successive versions of the Flexible Flow Management Program, most recently the 2017 FFMP. The FFMP is a staged, storage-indexed rule structure. Combined NYC storage is compared daily against seasonally varying threshold curves that define operating zones ranging from flood-mitigation levels through normal operations to three drought stages. Each zone prescribes diversion curtailments for NYC and NJ and scaling factors on each reservoir's minimum release, modulated by seasonal release profiles, and additional flood-mitigation releases apply above the highest storage zones. The central operational tension is structural. During severe drought, NYC's authorized diversion, the Montague compensating releases, and downstream ecological flows cannot all be met simultaneously, while wet-period storage management trades flood mitigation against drought reserve. This governance structure supplies a natural many-objective formulation. The FFMP's parameterized rules, which consist of negotiated quantities such as release baselines, drought curtailment factors, and storage-zone boundaries, supply a decision space whose re-optimization is institutionally meaningful because the rule structure is preserved and only its negotiated constants are varied (Section 3.1).

### 2.2 Simulation model

All policy evaluations use Pywr-DRB, an open-source daily water-availability and drought-risk model of the basin built on the Pywr simulation framework (Hamilton et al., 2024). Pywr-DRB represents the four NYC reservoirs and the major lower-basin reservoirs, the FFMP rule structure with its storage zones, seasonal release profiles, drought curtailments, and flood-mitigation releases, the Montague and Trenton flow targets with their time-of-travel lags, and NYC and NJ deliveries under the Decree's running-average accounting.

During optimization we use a trimmed configuration of the model in which the non-NYC reservoirs are simulated once under their default rules and their releases are supplied to the trimmed model as fixed boundary inflows. Each function evaluation therefore solves only the NYC-relevant portion of the network. This simplification is admissible because the decision variables (Section 3.1) alter only NYC operations, and the lower-basin reservoirs' rules do not respond to NYC decisions in the model. Because the boundary releases are policy-independent, they are computed once per realization and reused for every policy evaluated on that realization; the held-out re-evaluation (Section 6) therefore also runs the trimmed configuration, with a single presimulation pass over the test ensemble. The full model is used only to generate the boundary releases and to simulate the status-quo baseline policy on the historical trace. Verification that the trimmed model reproduces full-model objective values under matched inputs is reported in Supporting Information Section S1.

## 3. Problem Formulation

### 3.1 Decision variables

The decision vector parameterizes the 2017 FFMP rule structure with 39 variables. These comprise NYC diversion curtailment factors for the three drought stages, NJ curtailment factors for the two deepest stages, twelve vertical adjustments to the storage-zone threshold curves (two per curve — each curve is a trapezoid over the year, and the two shifts move its low storage plateau, the fall–winter void, and its high plateau, the spring–summer refill target, independently), six temporal shifts that slide each curve as a whole along the day-of-year axis, four seasonal scaling factors on the daily minimum-release profiles of the conservation zones, six scaling factors on the Montague and Trenton drought-stage flow-target factor tables (one per gauge and drought stage), and six scaling factors on the flood-zone (L1-a and L1-b) spill-mitigation release schedules of FFMP Tables 4a–4g, one per zone and reservoir. Restricting each curve to two plateau shifts and one temporal shift keeps the adjustment legible to DRBC stakeholders — each knob maps to a visible flat segment of the rule curve — while letting the zone geometry, including the conditional seasonal storage objective and its mandated autumn–winter void, be deepened or relaxed independently of the spring refill target and retimed. Every curve retains its trapezoidal shape, since the two ramps re-interpolate between the shifted plateaus, so no new kink dates are introduced. The flood-release scales are season-invariant by design, matching the FFMP, which holds these rows constant across its tables and seasons; the formulation's seasonal flood policy instead enters through the zone-boundary shifts, mirroring the FFMP's own structure, in which seasonal flood mitigation is encoded in the storage-zone geometry rather than the release rates. The seasonal minimum-release scales use the FFMP's own season definitions from the Tables 4a–4g headers (winter December–March, spring April–May, summer June–August, fall September–November); because the tables' finer date bins nest within these seasons, seasonally scaled schedules change value only on the FFMP's own bin boundaries. Three quantity classes are held fixed rather than optimized: the 1954 Decree quantities (the Montague and Trenton flow targets and the NYC diversion cap); the reservoir minimum-release baselines, which the FFMP itself never varies — operational variation enters through the forecast-based table selection that the seasonal scaling factors emulate; and the FFMP's maximum combined discharge rates (Table 5: 4,200, 2,400, and 3,400 cfs at Cannonsville, Pepacton, and Neversink), which are physical and regulatory limits on the release works and spillway at which scheduled flood-zone releases are capped. Bounds are set from the FFMP's own structure and operational evidence: the seasonal release scales span the FFMP's Table 4a–4g forecast-based envelope (0.8–2.6 of the base schedule); the L1-a flood-release scales are bounded above by the maximum controlled release observed in 2000–2021 at each reservoir, a demonstrated release-works capacity; the NJ curtailment bounds bracket the negotiated FFMP values; and the flow-target scales are capped where the Decree-target ceiling binds. Feasibility is enforced at evaluation time by monotonicity clamps — storage-zone curves may not cross, deeper drought stages may not permit larger diversions, and flood-zone releases obey L1-a ≥ L1-b — so every simulated policy is operationally coherent. Two of these conditions, delivery-curtailment monotonicity and the flood-zone release ordering, are additionally posed to the optimizer as formal constraints computed from decision-variable arithmetic alone, so infeasible vectors are identified and discarded under constraint-dominance before any simulation is spent on them; the zone-boundary conditions remain clamp-only, because the clamped geometry is itself the intended policy. The FFMP's structural logic, including its storage-indexed stages, seasonal profiles, and running-average delivery accounting, is preserved exactly.

For RQ3, a variable-resolution generalization (denoted FFMP-*n*) interpolates the storage-zone structure to *n* zones and adds curtailment and shift variables per zone, sweeping *n* over the values 8, 10, and 12. Because the sweep multiplies search cost, it is scheduled against the compute reserve remaining at the end of the campaign rather than run under every design, and the scenario design under which it runs is decided if and when that reserve permits (Section 7).

### 3.2 Objectives

The objective set spans the stakeholder priorities that the basin's governance encodes: NYC supply, the Decree flow obligations protecting New Jersey and Philadelphia, downstream flood exposure, and reservoir-system resilience. Eight objectives are active (Table 2). The eighth, NJ delivery reliability, was activated after a redundancy screen over 512 constraint-feasible policies on each campaign search ensemble showed it is not collinear with any other objective (Spearman |ρ| ≤ 0.38 everywhere, and ≤ 0.08 with Trenton flow reliability; Supporting Information Section S6), so New Jersey's Decree delivery interest carries independent information the remaining objectives do not. All metrics are computed on the metric window of each simulation — the daily output from six calendar months after its start, cut by date — and with weekly accounting following the Decree convention. Six months is the accumulation period the SSI-6 drought index requires before its first defined value, so the hazard metrics that select scenarios exclude the same interval and selection and evaluation score the identical window. Compliance is always scored against static goalposts — the 1954 Decree quantities of 800 MGD for NYC, 1,131.05 MGD at Montague, and 100 MGD for NJ, together with the FFMP's Trenton equivalent-flow objective of 1,938.95 MGD — and never against the FFMP's live drought-adjusted targets. Scoring against the live target would allow a policy to satisfy its obligations by triggering the drought step-downs that lower those obligations. For NYC and NJ supply the Decree limits the running *average* of diversion rather than any single day's, so daily demand is not clipped at the static right; instead each day's delivery target is the realizable entitlement — the smaller of that day's demand and the running-average allowance banked to it under the FFMP delivery accounting — reconstructed at the static right so that a policy's own drought step-downs cannot lower it. A demand spike above the flat daily right is therefore honored whenever prior under-use has banked the allowance for it, while demand beyond the banked right is not counted as owed.

Two formulation choices depart from common practice deliberately, following the objective-stability findings of Quinn et al. (2017) and the convergence findings of Bonham et al. (2024). First, tail objectives use the conditional value-at-risk at the 90 percent level (CVaR90), defined as the mean of the worst decile of weekly deficits (Rockafellar and Uryasev, 2000; Löhndorf, 2016; Fairbrother et al., 2022), rather than the single worst week, which Quinn et al. (2017) identify as a high-variance, low-information signal. Second, flood exposure is the mean annual count of days above the National Weather Service minor flood stage at three vulnerable gauges (Hale Eddy, Fishs Eddy, and Bridgeville), normalized per metric-window year so that the metric is invariant to record length. This count-over-threshold form avoids the known pathologies of expected-damage objectives (Quinn et al., 2017). Storage resilience uses the fifth percentile of aggregate NYC storage rather than the single-day minimum. Trenton flow reliability stands in for salt-front position because the Trenton target exists to repel the salt front, which makes the two physically redundant, and because the flow form avoids reliance on a statistical salinity emulator in the extreme droughts where objective values are decided.

### 3.3 Across-realization aggregation during search

For the single-trace historic design the temporal metrics of Table 2 are the objectives. Each ensemble design must additionally collapse a matrix of objective values across realizations to one scalar per objective at each function evaluation. We use a two-layer annual-unit scheme. Each realization's metric-window series is divided into water-year units. Scenario windows are October-aligned, so the window opens on April 1 of the first water year and the first whole water-year unit is the second water year. The first stage computes each objective's annual metric on every realization-year unit, and the second stage aggregates across the pooled *N*(*L*−1) unit-years with a per-objective unit operator,

$$
J_i \;=\; \Psi_i\big[\,\{\varphi_i(r, y)\}_{r=1..N,\; y=1..L-1}\,\big],
$$

where $\varphi_i$ is objective *i*'s annual metric and $\Psi_i$ is its unit operator. The reliability objectives (1, 3, 5, and 8) use an annual failure indicator, which registers a failure when at least *k* weeks in the unit-year violate the corresponding goalpost (*k* = 3 for NYC delivery and Montague flow, *k* = 1 for Trenton and NJ delivery; the failure-week count is a convention, screened for saturation by the framing-convention diagnostics with the adopted values inside the screen grid), and take $\Psi$ as the frequency of non-failure years. This satisficing-frequency form is the search-time reliability operator used throughout the regional water-supply lineage (Zeff et al., 2014; Trindade et al., 2017; Gold et al., 2023). The deficit objectives (2 and 4) compute the CVaR90 deficit within each unit-year, and the storage objective (7) the annual minimum of daily aggregate storage; all three take $\Psi$ as the worst first-percentile unit-year, the stable tail form of Quinn et al. (2017, 2018). The flood-days objective (6) takes $\Psi$ as the mean across unit-years, which corresponds to expected annual flood days. A 99th-percentile variant was registered because expectations can mask floods (Quinn et al., 2017), and the operator choice was screened rather than assumed: at the campaign's pooled denominator of 900 unit-years the 99th-percentile form collapses onto two or three integer day counts across the policy population (interquartile range near zero), is an order of magnitude noisier under bootstrap, and induces an unstable policy ranking, so the mean was adopted and the percentile form retained as a reported diagnostic (Supporting Information Section S6). The historic record is scored as consecutive annual units with inherited state, following Quinn et al. (2018), so it carries the same unit denominator per realization-year as the ensemble designs (76 metric-bearing unit-years on its 77-year trace; unit boundaries are scoring windows, not restarts). Unit-years within a realization are serially dependent, since consecutive failure-years are precisely how multi-year droughts express themselves in this scheme. The effective sample size is therefore below *N*(*L*−1), which is disclosed rather than corrected (Section 8). At the campaign size (*N* = 100, *L* = 10) the pooled denominator is 900 unit-years, comparable to the 1,000 one-year members that Quinn et al. (2017, 2018) use to stabilize the same worst-first-percentile operator, which is the ground for a per-evaluation ensemble smaller than the 1,000-realization convention of the Zeff–Herman–Trindade lineage (Section 5.1).

Two properties of this scheme matter for the comparison. First, the temporal metrics of Table 2 and the entire held-out re-evaluation metric set (Section 6) are identical across designs. Only this across-realization stage differs, and it differs only because the two ensemble designs present different scenario compositions to it. Second, cross-design commensurability during search is neither required nor claimed. A design that deliberately over-represents severe hazard conditions computes search objectives under a distribution that differs from the generating process (Section 4.5), and the held-out re-evaluation is the only comparison point.

## 4. Scenario Designs for the Search Ensemble

### 4.1 The three designs

We compare three constructions of the search ensemble, all drawn from a single stationary population and all evaluated at matched size and record length except where a design cannot be matched by construction.

The first design, `historic`, is the observed record simulated as one continuous trace, with one realization of length 77 years. It is the reference for prevailing applied practice (Giuliani et al., 2016; Herman et al., 2020). It cannot be matched in ensemble size or budget and is reported as a reference rather than entered into the controlled contrast.

The second design, `fixed_probabilistic`, consists of *N* realizations of length *L* drawn independently and identically from the stationary stochastic generator and frozen for the entire search. This is the standard construction of the regional water-supply lineage (Quinn et al., 2017; Zatarain Salazar et al., 2017) and the reference against which designed selection is judged. It is the discipline's default, and the contrast between it and the third design is the core of RQ1.

The third design, `hazard_filling`, is the proposed method. It selects *N* realizations from a large candidate pool by space-filling coverage of the hydrologic hazard space, using the selector of Section 4.4. It is constructed from the same stationary generator as `fixed_probabilistic`, at the same *N* and *L*, so that the two designs differ only in the rule that selects which realizations enter the search ensemble.

The stationary population is the flow model of the prevailing water-supply optimization lineage (Herman et al., 2014; Trindade et al., 2017; Zatarain Salazar et al., 2017; Gold et al., 2023). Hazard variation within it arises entirely from natural variability, in that some 10-year windows contain severe multi-year droughts and most do not. Restricting the search to this population is deliberate. It isolates the question of RQ1, which is whether the rule that selects realizations from a fixed generating process matters, without confounding that rule with a change in the generating process itself. The deeply uncertain climate-forcing space enters the study only in the held-out test ensemble (Section 6.1), where it makes the re-evaluation a test of generalization to conditions absent from search. Prior work supports hazard-oriented selection within a stationary population: Zatarain Salazar et al. (2017) stratify a stationary pool by a realized-flow statistic, and Herman et al. (2016) amplify drought severity with a stationary weighted bootstrap.

### 4.2 The generator and the candidate pool

Daily multi-site realizations are generated by the Kirsch–Nowak pipeline. Cholesky-based monthly generation preserves cross-site and lag correlations (Kirsch et al., 2013), and a nearest-neighbour resampling scheme disaggregates monthly flows to daily flows (Nowak et al., 2010). The generator is fitted to the reconstructed observed record (1945–2022) and run without climate perturbation.

Both ensemble designs draw from this one generator, and the distinction between them is the mechanism of selection. Because the generator's parameters are a knob, a design can generate as many independent realizations as it wants, but it cannot instruct the generator to produce a realization at a prescribed drought severity. Hazard coordinates are emergent properties of a realized sequence, computed only after the sequence exists. A design that targets hazard coverage must therefore select realizations from a finite pool of already-generated sequences, rather than generate directly to a target. The nearest-neighbour step in the selector (Section 4.4) is a consequence of this fact, not an approximation of a preferable procedure. This is a scope choice given a fixed fitted generator: a generator purpose-built to emit sequences at prescribed hazard targets is an alternative construction (Zaniolo et al., 2023, 2024), which we do not pursue because it forgoes the simulation-free scalability of selecting from a pool and requires committing to a small number of discrete target types.

The `hazard_filling` design owns a candidate pool of *P* = 10⁶ realizations sampled independently from the stationary generator. The pool size is set by the pool-size diagnostic of Section 4.4 — tail resolution in the selection space improves with *P* — and is essentially free to enlarge: generating the pool involves no system simulation, and a 10⁶-member pool costs roughly 600 core-hours, under two percent of the campaign allocation across its replicated re-rolls. The pool is never simulated. Only each realization's hazard coordinates and generation seed are stored, and the *N* selected members are regenerated exactly on demand from a deterministic, globally indexed random-stream architecture (Supporting Information Section S3). This storage arrangement is what makes million-member pools tractable.

The requirement that the candidate pool be sampled independently, rather than by a structured design such as Latin hypercube, is essential to the validity of the comparison. A uniform random subset of size *N* taken from an independent and identically distributed pool has exactly the joint distribution of *N* fresh independent draws. This distributional equivalence is what makes `fixed_probabilistic` the exact statistical control for `hazard_filling`: the two designs then differ only in the rule that selects *N* members from the same population law, and any difference in re-evaluated robustness is attributable to that rule alone. The condition is enforced by an automated invariant in the study's code, because no other component of the pipeline would fail if it were violated.

### 4.3 Realizations, windows, and hazard metrics

Every ensemble scenario is a disjoint, non-overlapping *L*-year realization. Each simulation starts from a fixed initial storage of 80 percent of capacity, which constitutes a terminating-simulation design, and the first six months of each window are excluded from all metrics. A drought event that would straddle a window boundary is split by it. The choice of *L* bounds this truncation (Section 5.1), and event-scale severity is additionally captured at re-evaluation, where records are at least as long.

Hazard coordinates are computed on the aggregate inflow to the three NYC reservoirs before any system simulation. The candidate axes are drought-event metrics derived from run theory applied to the six-month Standardized Streamflow Index, consisting of the deficit volume, duration, peak depth, onset rate, and recovery rate of the controlling event (McKee et al., 1993; Vicente-Serrano et al., 2012), together with flood metrics from a peaks-over-threshold analysis of daily flows, consisting of the peak magnitude, pulse duration, and rise rate. The event models are fixed as follows. A drought event is a run of SSI-6 below zero that reaches −1 or lower, terminated by three consecutive non-negative months; when a window contains more than one event, the *controlling* event — the one with the largest cumulative deficit, the operationally binding event under fixed initial storage — is scored, and all five dry descriptors are zero for a window with no qualifying event. On the wet side, the high-flow threshold is the 95th percentile of the observed record's daily flow, and the *critical pulse* is the exceedance run containing the window's maximum daily flow, so exactly one pulse is scored per window and no declustering parameter is required: peak magnitude is the window maximum normalized by the observed mean daily flow (always defined), pulse duration is the number of days above threshold within that run, and rise rate is the largest one-day rise on the pulse's rising limb, likewise normalized. The SSI distribution fit, the flood threshold, and the normalizing mean are all fixed once on the observed record and reused for every realization, so hazard coordinates are comparable across the pool and the historic reference.

Two scope properties of this hazard space are disclosed rather than remedied. First, the axes are defined on aggregate NYC inflow only: the flows deciding the Montague, Trenton, and flood-gauge objectives include unregulated tributary contributions the hazard image never sees, and a Supporting Information check correlates selected-ensemble hazard coordinates against realized stress at the downstream objective sites. Second, the descriptors characterize a single controlling event per window: no frequency, timing, or inter-event descriptor survives to the selection, so a window with two moderate droughts and a window with one are indistinguishable in this coordinate system. Event multiplicity is left as an explicit extension.

The descriptor set is screened on the pool's hazard image before selection. Degenerate descriptors with near-zero spread are dropped, and near-duplicate pairs, with absolute Spearman rank correlation of at least 0.95, are pruned to a single canonical member so that one hazard concept cannot enter the selection distance twice under two names. On this population the screen retains all eight candidate descriptors: the largest pairwise rank correlation, 0.88 between deficit volume and duration, is below the pruning cut, is stable across pool sizes from 2×10³ to 10⁶ members, and the retained set is insensitive to tightening the cut to 0.90. Six of the retained descriptors serve as the selection axes — deficit volume, peak depth, onset rate, and recovery rate on the drought side, and peak magnitude and pulse duration on the flood side. The two remaining descriptors are excluded from the selection distance: drought duration is the most redundant member of the retained set (rank correlation 0.87 with deficit volume, with a quasi-discrete, months-valued tail), and flood rise rate is the flood descriptor most entangled with the retained pair (rank correlation 0.60 with peak magnitude). Limiting the selection space matters because the selector's reach into the severe corners weakens as axes are added at fixed pool size (Section 4.4). Both excluded descriptors are still computed for every pool member and reported as post-hoc diagnostics, and the redundancy structure of the descriptor set, characterized following the index-redundancy logic of Olden and Poff (2003), is reported in Supporting Information Section S4, together with a selection-invariance check that quantifies the weighting that correlated axes induce in the selection distance.

### 4.4 The hazard-filling selector

The selector fills the selection space — the six hazard axes of Section 4.3 — in absolute, range-scaled magnitude units. Each selection axis is scaled to the unit interval by its robust pool range — the per-axis 1st and 99th percentiles, with members beyond them clipped to the box faces — so that no single axis dominates distances while the spacing within each axis remains proportional to physical magnitude. Robust percentile bounds, rather than the sample extremes, are what make the selection geometry a stable functional of the population: the sample minimum and maximum of a right-skewed hazard metric are non-convergent extreme order statistics, so a full-range box would grow with the pool size *P* (tying the strength of the tail distortion to a nuisance sizing parameter), vary materially across the *K* pool re-rolls (breaking draw commensurability), and allow a single outlier to compress the bulk of the pool into a corner of the box. The clipped mass is bounded at one percent per axis tail and is reported, with the realized bounds, as a build diagnostic; a bounds sweep confirms the choice empirically, with full-range scaling degrading realized coverage roughly threefold through outlier fixation while coverage and enrichment vary smoothly across nearby robust-percentile settings (Supporting Information Section S5). *N* Latin hypercube anchors are drawn in the scaled hazard box, and each anchor is snapped to the nearest not-yet-selected pool member in Euclidean distance. The selector is deterministic given its anchor seed. It involves no iterative optimization of a coverage criterion and no tuning parameters. Because Latin hypercube anchors stratify every axis marginally regardless of dimension, the selector's guarantee is stratified marginal coverage with tail enrichment on each selection axis; joint coverage is reported descriptively against a random design of the same size and dimension (Supporting Information Section S5) rather than claimed as uniform filling.

The strength of the enrichment the selector achieves is set jointly by the pool size and the dimension of the selection space. The expected distance from an anchor to its nearest pool member scales as $P^{-1/m}$ on $m$ axes, so at fixed pool size each added axis dilutes the snap and pulls selected members back toward the bulk of the pool; in the coverage diagnostics, mean snap distance roughly triples between four- and eight-axis selection on the same pool, with per-axis tail enrichment falling accordingly. The design responds on both margins: the selection space is held to the six axes of Section 4.3 rather than the full descriptor set, and the pool size is set at $P = 10^6$ from a nested pool-size diagnostic in which the selection is repeated on nested prefixes of a single large pool — honest i.i.d. pools of every intermediate size under the global-index seed architecture. At the production configuration, every selection axis places roughly three times the unbiased share of selected members in its upper decile (at least 31 percent of selected members above the pool's 90th percentile, against the 10 percent that i.i.d. sampling delivers), and this realized enrichment is re-measured on every production draw as a build diagnostic (Supporting Information Section S5).

Absolute-magnitude scaling is the operative choice, and it is what makes the design a genuine intervention rather than a variance-reduction tweak. Because the hazard marginals of a stochastic generator are strongly right-skewed, most pool members cluster near the center of the hazard space and few occupy the severe corners. Filling the range uniformly therefore draws selected members from the sparse corners far more often than their pool frequency, so the severe drought and flood conditions that decide reservoir performance are over-represented in the search ensemble relative to their probability under the generator. This is the deliberate distribution shift that RQ1 tests. An alternative that transforms each axis by its empirical cumulative distribution before filling would instead reproduce the pool's marginal frequencies and distort only the joint dependence among axes; that rank-space variant is registered as a non-campaign sensitivity, because it isolates how much of any effect is attributable to tail over-representation specifically, but it is not the campaign design.

The rationale for uniform coverage of a condition space, as opposed to probability-faithful sampling of it, descends from the bottom-up and decision-scaling traditions, which sample condition spaces uniformly to map system response rather than to estimate expectations (Brown et al., 2012; Culley et al., 2016; Herman et al., 2016; Fowler et al., 2024). We take from that tradition the coverage argument for constructing the ensemble, and not a precedent for biasing a reported robustness number. The selector is deliberately the simplest space-filling design so that its construction variance is measurable under replication (Section 5.2) and so that coverage statistics remain an independent verification of what the selector achieved, reported as a method diagnostic rather than as a comparison result (Supporting Information Section S5).

### 4.5 The consequence of the distribution shift

The two ensemble designs present different scenario distributions to the search. `fixed_probabilistic` presents the generator's own distribution, and `hazard_filling` presents a distribution deliberately shifted toward severe hazard conditions. Any objective aggregated over the search ensemble is therefore computed under a different measure for the two designs, and for `hazard_filling` that measure over-weights the conditions in which policies are hardest to satisfy. The shift biases which policies the optimizer selects, not merely a reported number, which is exactly why it is a design intervention worth testing and why search objectives are never compared across designs.

The held-out re-evaluation removes the evaluation bias of scoring each design on its own ensemble, because both designs are scored on the same instrument. It does not remove, and is not intended to remove, the selection bias that the hazard-filling construction embodies. That selection bias is the design effect under study. Whether over-representing severe hazard conditions during search yields policies that are more robust under held-out deeply uncertain re-evaluation, or whether it merely tailors policies to conditions that re-evaluation does not reward, is the empirical question, and the common test ensemble is what makes the two outcomes distinguishable. Importance-sampling reweighting of the search objectives back to the generator's measure is not attempted. The snapped-selection rule induces no tractable density, so exact weights are not available; and even an approximate reweighting would defeat the purpose of the intervention, which is to let severe conditions drive the search.

## 5. Experimental Design

### 5.1 Budget control and sizing

Both matched designs use *N* = 100 realizations of *L* = 10 years, which amounts to 1,000 scenario-years per function evaluation, and both run at equal NFE. Because *N* and *L* are common, the per-evaluation simulation cost, scenario-years, and wall-clock time are identical across designs. Equal NFE and equal scenario-years therefore coincide, giving one budget condition and no confound between ensemble composition and search effort.

The common *N* and *L* are required rather than convenient. If *L* differed across designs, the selection rule would be confounded with record length, and at a fixed per-evaluation budget *N* and *L* trade off directly. The value of *N* is bounded below by the coverage requirement. The Latin hypercube anchors stratify each selection axis into *N* bins, so marginal coverage and tail resolution sharpen with *N*, and the achieved per-axis and joint coverage are reported against a random design of the same size and dimension so that the realized coverage is demonstrated rather than assumed (Supporting Information Section S5). The coverage diagnostics support *N* = 100 directly: every selection axis is better stratified and more tail-enriched than a random design of the same size, and enlarging *N* at fixed pool size lowers per-axis tail enrichment rather than raising it, because the pool holds only *P*/10 members above the 90th percentile of any axis and additional selections increasingly draw from the bulk. Campaign cost also scales linearly with *N*. A long-record design, for example *L* = 50 years forcing *N* to approximately 20, would leave too few anchors for meaningful stratification. The value *L* = 10 years in turn exceeds the duration of the DRB drought of record in the 1960s, which lasted four to five years, plus its onset and recovery, so a design-basis event fits within a single window and duration-type hazard axes are not truncation-limited. The per-evaluation ensemble of 100 realizations is smaller than the 1,000-realization convention of the Zeff–Herman–Trindade lineage; the ground for it is the annual-unit pooling of Section 3.3, which yields 900 metric-bearing unit-years per evaluation, comparable to the sample on which that lineage stabilizes the same tail operators. The sampling variance of the worst-first-percentile operator at *N* = 100 is quantified by the epsilon-calibration bootstrap (Supporting Information Section S6). The `historic` design, with one realization of about 77 years, cannot be budget-matched and is reported as a reference throughout.

The function-evaluation budget is 500,000 NFE per search, which is within the range used for comparable reservoir control-policy problems in this lineage (Quinn et al., 2017; Bartholomew and Kwakkel, 2020). Because the runtime archive (Section 5.3) records intermediate NFE levels, the attained budget is justified against observed convergence behavior after the fact, and the design comparison is recomputable at earlier budgets at re-evaluation cost only.

### 5.2 Replication: ensemble draws and search seeds

Two sources of variability are separated by design. A draw is a scenario design's entire construction re-run from scratch with a fresh seed. For `fixed_probabilistic` a draw is a fresh i.i.d. sample, and for `hazard_filling` a draw is a fresh candidate pool together with a fresh anchor plan. Re-drawing the pool within each draw is essential to the comparison. Generating the pool is part of the hazard-filling construction, and pinning it across draws would make hazard filling appear more stable than `fixed_probabilistic` as an artifact of the replication scheme rather than as a finding. A seed is an independent MOEA trial on a fixed draw. Each matched design runs *K* = 3 draws with *S* = 2 seeds per draw, set against the compute allocation. The `historic` design has *K* = 1, because its composition variance is zero by construction, and is replicated across *S* seeds only.

The unit of analysis for the between-design comparison is the draw, and seeds within a draw are treated as pseudoreplicates; draw- and seed-level results are reported transparently, so the design effect is read against the observed construction and search variability. This replication structure separates ensemble-construction variance from search stochasticity. It is the feature that distinguishes the present comparison from the antecedent that most resembles it, Eker and Kwakkel (2018), which compared a single designed scenario set against a single random set by counting solutions and could not separate a genuine design effect from the variance of a single draw.

### 5.3 Convergence reporting

Within each design, search progress is monitored through the algorithm's runtime dynamics and reported per seed as a diagnostic, following the runtime-metrics protocol of Reed et al. (2013) and the ε-progress and hypervolume conventions of Hadka and Reed (2015). These internal quantities are never compared across designs, because objective values computed on different search ensembles are not commensurable, and because reference-set metrics scored against a pooled frontier are biased in a design-dependent way (Section 6.5). Convergence assessment is not used as a stopping rule, so no design benefits from a tuned termination criterion, and both matched designs execute the identical NFE budget. Because the runtime archive records the approximation set at intermediate NFE levels, the final design comparison (Section 6) can be recomputed at two or three earlier budgets at re-evaluation cost only, which tests whether the design ranking is an artifact of the chosen budget rather than a stable result.

## 6. Held-Out Re-Evaluation and Comparison Metrics

### 6.1 The test ensemble E_test

Every design's final Pareto-approximate set is re-simulated, with the trimmed model used in search (Section 2; the policy-independent boundary releases are presimulated once per test realization and reused for every policy), on a single common test ensemble E_test, and all cross-design comparison is made there. E_test is never used during any search, is never the source of any search ensemble, and is generated from a reserved seed domain disjoint from that of the candidate pool. A collision between the search-side and test-side seed domains is treated as a hard error, which guards against the failure mode of testing on data the search has seen (Bonham et al., 2024).

E_test is the largest ensemble in the study by a wide margin, and it is built to encompass a deeply uncertain climate-forcing envelope rather than to represent a probability distribution. The forcing is a monthly change factor applied to the generator's fitted moments, parameterized as a low-order harmonic in the log change factor whose amplitudes govern annual volume, seasonal amplitude, and the snowmelt-shoulder shape, with bounds taken from a CMIP6 multimodel ensemble (Supporting Information Section S2). The adjustment preserves each month's coefficient of variation, so the forcing perturbs the mean seasonal profile without independently altering relative variability. E_test is a Latin hypercube over the full empirical range of the CMIP6-fitted amplitudes, widened by a 25 percent margin — an envelope deliberately wider than any variation the search ensembles contain — crossed with many stochastic realizations per Latin hypercube point. Each Latin hypercube point is a state of the world, and its *R*_test realizations sample natural variability within that state. E_test comprises *N*_θ,test = 1,000 states of the world × *R*_test = 25 realizations × *L*_test = 50 years — 25,000 realizations and 1.25 million scenario-years. The state count fixes the Monte Carlo resolution of any reported satisficing fraction at ±1.6 percentage points in the worst case. Each state's *R*_test × (*L*_test − 1) = 1,225 metric-bearing annual units match the ~900–1,000 unit-year sample at which this lineage's tail operators stabilize (Section 7), so the within-state performance estimate is resolved at least as well as one search evaluation. The 50-year records, five times the search-record length, additionally test sustained operation: consecutive droughts are experienced on carried-over storage and the running-average delivery accounting matures, neither of which a terminating 10-year window can express. Where E_test itself is placed in the hazard space — the coverage overlay that locates its forced droughts relative to the natural-variability corners the selector enriches — its hazard coordinates are computed on disjoint, October-aligned 10-year sub-windows, so they are commensurable with the pool's window convention. Adequacy of both sample sizes is verified after re-evaluation by state- and realization-subsample stability of the design rankings, scored offline from the persisted matrix (Section 6.2) at no additional simulation cost. This construction, a Latin hypercube of states of the world crossed with stochastic realizations, is the standard of the re-evaluation lineage (Kasprzyk et al., 2013; Trindade et al., 2017; Bartholomew and Kwakkel, 2020; Gold et al., 2022; Quinn et al., 2020).

Two properties of E_test carry the interpretation of the comparison. First, the search ensembles are drawn from the unperturbed stationary generator, whereas E_test spans a forced climate envelope that no search ensemble contains. The re-evaluation therefore measures whether hazard-space coverage of the natural-variability manifold produces policies that generalize to conditions absent from search, which is a stronger test than re-evaluating on the search distribution and which keeps the test instrument structurally distinct from both search designs. Second, no robustness number reported in this study is an expectation. Under deep uncertainty there is no probability measure over the forcing space, which is what the term deep denotes (Lamontagne et al., 2018). A satisficing fraction computed over E_test is a coverage-weighted count over a designed exploration of the deeply uncertain box, and it is reported as such rather than as an estimate of an expected value. The cross-design comparison is commensurable because E_test is identical across designs, not because it is probability-faithful.

Following Quinn et al. (2020), we do not claim that E_test is scenario-neutral, because no experimental design is, and all design rankings are conditional on this one reference ensemble. With a single construction, that conditioning is declared rather than bounded. A second, structurally different construction, replacing the Kirsch–Nowak dependence structure with a hidden-Markov annual generator, is registered but sits outside the campaign: the basin-fitted transition structure is near-memoryless, so it would vary the generator family without adding a distinct stress, and it would stand up a second evaluation ensemble beside the pre-specified single-ensemble endpoint. The persisted re-evaluation matrix (Section 6.2) instead supports zero-simulation-cost composition-sensitivity checks, in which hazard-restricted and envelope-restricted subsets of the single E_test are re-scored to probe whether the ranking depends on the region of the test space emphasized. We note that the default construction inherits the historical record's interannual wet-dry persistence, so persistence is stressed in neither the search population nor the test ensemble; Section 8 scopes the claims accordingly.

### 6.2 The persisted re-evaluation matrix

Re-evaluation persists the complete array of objective values, indexed by solution, realization, and objective, in natural units, together with each realization's state-of-the-world identifier. Every robustness metric in Section 6.3 is then an offline re-scoring of this array, and no metric choice, threshold change, or new metric requires re-simulation. In the taxonomy of McPhail et al. (2018), the persisted array is the substrate on which any performance transformation, scenario subset, and aggregation can be recomposed after the fact. Both natural units of robustness are thereby available. The realization unit applies the criterion across all *N*_θ,test × *R*_test realizations. The state-of-the-world unit of the regional-supply lineage collapses each forcing point's realizations first, by a stated within-state aggregator, and then applies the criterion across the *N*_θ,test states (Herman et al., 2014; Trindade et al., 2017; Gold et al., 2022, 2023). Precision is governed by *N*_θ,test rather than by the raw realization count, because realizations within a state are not independent, so uncertainty on ensemble-level quantities is assessed at the state-of-the-world level.

### 6.3 Comparison metrics

#### 6.3.1 The primary endpoint

The comparison endpoint for RQ1 is the re-evaluated multivariate satisficing fraction of the policies a design produces, under Starr's domain criterion. For a single policy, the satisficing fraction on E_test is

$$
S(x) \;=\; \frac{1}{N_{\text{test}}} \sum_{j=1}^{N_{\text{test}}} \Lambda_j(x),
\qquad
\Lambda_j(x) \;=\; \prod_{i} \mathbf{1}\!\left[\, f_{i}(x, s_j) \;\text{meets}\; c_i \,\right],
$$

where $f_i(x, s_j)$ is policy *x*'s whole-trace temporal metric (Table 2) for objective *i* on test realization $s_j$, and $c_i$ is that objective's criterion. This is Starr's (1962) domain criterion, the standard robustness measure of the regional water-supply lineage (Herman et al., 2014, 2015; Trindade et al., 2017; Gold et al., 2022, 2023), and the fastest-converging robustness family in ensemble-size testing, with accurate rankings from 50 to 300 scenarios (Bonham et al., 2024).

A single search yields a Pareto-approximate set rather than one policy, so a run-level scalar is required before the between-design comparison of Section 5.2 can operate. The pre-specified run-level endpoint is the maximum satisficing fraction attained by any policy in the run's re-evaluated set, which answers the practical question of whether a design can find a robust policy at all. A maximum over a set inherits a mild upward bias that grows with set cardinality, which is disclosed, and the per-objective satisficing decomposition is reported alongside so that a single scalar does not carry the entire comparison.

The criteria $c_i$ are anchored on Decree and FFMP goalposts wherever one exists and are otherwise conventions, which is why they are swept (Section 6.4) rather than fixed at a single point. [Open item: the criterion anchor values.]

#### 6.3.2 Secondary metrics

Four secondary metrics are reported alongside the primary endpoint, all as offline re-scorings of the persisted matrix. Univariate satisficing gives the per-objective decomposition of the primary. The coverage-weighted mean across realizations is the risk-neutral reference, and the worst realization is the risk-averse reference; both are reported because metric choice is known to change robustness rankings (Herman et al., 2015; Giuliani and Castelletti, 2016; McPhail et al., 2018). The signed improvement over the status quo is the orientation-signed difference between each policy and the default 2017 FFMP policy simulated on the same E_test, which answers RQ2 by measuring whether re-optimization improves on current operations, and which serves as a fixed, design-independent reference in the deviation-from-baseline tradition of Kasprzyk et al. (2013). This last metric is a fixed-reference regret in the transformation taxonomy of McPhail et al. (2018); it is design-independent and does not move when designs are added or dropped, which is the property that a set-relative regret lacks (Section 6.3.3). Agreement among the design rankings induced by these metrics is summarized with Kendall's rank correlation τ_b (McPhail et al., 2020), and because a robustness scalar can be stable, optimizable, and still misleading (Huang et al., 2025), the raw performance distribution on E_test, summarized by its median and spread, is co-reported alongside every robustness value.

#### 6.3.3 Metrics deliberately excluded

No set-relative regret measure is computed. Best-in-set regret defines the reference by the best-performing policy in each scenario, so removing one design from the pooled set changes every other design's score, which violates the minimum requirement that a cross-design statistic be design-independent. It is also the slowest-converging metric family in Bonham et al. (2024), requiring more than 400 scenarios under their accuracy criterion and failing to converge within their tested range on a maximum-over-time objective, and our two deficit objectives are worst-first-percentile operators of exactly that type. Baseline regret in the sense of Cohen et al. (2021) requires one perfect-foresight optimization per scenario, which amounted to 3,233 CPU-hours in total across their 97 traces, is formulation-specific, and does not scale to the million-member candidate pools used here; we cite it as motivation and never compute it. No search-minus-test overfitting gap is reported: Brodeur et al. (2020) diagnose overfitting graphically and define no gap metric, and under the hazard-filling design the in-sample term is computed under a deliberately shifted distribution, so the difference between the in-sample and held-out terms is an artifact of the distribution change that would grow with exactly the coverage the method advocates.

#### 6.3.4 Attainability screen

From the persisted array we flag, at no additional simulation cost, the E_test realizations in which no policy from either design meets the criteria. This screen separates poor search performance from scenarios that are unwinnable for the evaluated policy pool. Shavazipour et al. (2021) found that 23 percent of their test scenarios were unwinnable by any policy. Each design's satisficing fraction is bounded above by the attainable fraction, which sets the ceiling against which the design difference is read. The screen provides an empirical bound over the evaluated policies rather than a per-scenario oracle, since an oracle would require exactly the perfect-foresight optimization that this study excludes.

### 6.4 The criterion sweep

No study in this lineage derives its satisficing criteria. Where a criterion is not fixed by a Decree or FFMP goalpost, it descends from stakeholder elicitations (Zeff et al., 2014) and is a convention. We therefore anchor each criterion on a goalpost where one exists and sweep the remainder over a grid, asking not whether robustness values move, which they will, but whether the design difference is invariant. The sweep is necessary rather than cosmetic. Quinn et al. (2020) show that robustness-rank agreement across experimental designs degrades as the satisficing criterion becomes more stringent, so a single fixed threshold could manufacture or hide the entire design effect. The sweep is implemented on a single stringency axis: at stringency *s*, each objective's criterion is placed at the oriented *s*-quantile of that objective's distribution pooled across all designs' re-evaluated policies on E_test, so that one value of *s* excludes the same marginal fraction of pooled outcomes for every objective and every design, and *s* is swept from lax to stringent with the Decree- and FFMP-anchored criteria located on the same axis. Pooling across designs is what makes equal stringency mean equal severity for every design. Threshold-margin diagnostics, consisting of each objective's cumulative distribution across E_test with the criterion overlaid, follow Gold et al. (2023) and accompany the sweep. This is the sense in which the comparison is reported as a difference that holds, or fails to hold, across the range of defensible criteria, rather than as a single number at one arbitrary threshold.

### 6.5 Reference sets

Where dominance-based summaries over the pooled re-evaluated policies are reported, they are read with three caveats stated up front: a design contributes points to the frontier against which it is scored, the two designs return different numbers of solutions (an asymmetry that Bartholomew and Kwakkel (2020) acknowledge but do not correct), and noisier re-evaluated estimates contribute more spuriously nondominated points (Shavazipour et al., 2021). Within-design MOEA search metrics, including hypervolume, are computed against per-design reference sets only and read purely as convergence diagnostics, following the protocol of Zatarain Salazar et al. (2017), who established that reference sets must be built per search-ensemble level and that cross-level comparison is defensible only on a common independent verification ensemble. These summaries are supplementary; the primary comparison is the endpoint of Section 6.3 on E_test.

## 7. Computational Experiment

All searches use the Multi-Master Borg MOEA (Hadka and Reed, 2013, 2015), whose epsilon-dominance archiving, adaptive operator selection, and randomized restarts have repeatedly proven reliable for many-objective water resources problems (Reed et al., 2013; Zatarain Salazar et al., 2016, 2017). The per-evaluation cost of the campaign ensemble configuration was measured directly on the Purdue Anvil system rather than extrapolated: at 128 evaluator ranks per node, one function evaluation over the campaign ensemble (100 realizations of 10 years on the trimmed model) takes a median of 173.8 s (the full model, used only for the presimulation passes and the historical baseline, is 1.16 times as expensive; Supporting Information Section S8). Combined with the measured strong-scaling efficiency of 0.73, one 500,000-NFE search runs for approximately 33 h on eight nodes (four islands of 254 evaluator ranks each, 1,021 MPI ranks in total) and costs approximately 33,400 service units. The matched campaign of two designs at three draws and two seeds each, together with the cheap `historic` reference, ensemble generation, and the trimmed-model re-evaluation of all final policies on E_test (~80,000 service units at the roughly 1,000–1,200 merged Pareto policies implied by the calibrated epsilon precisions), totals approximately 503,000 of the 750,000 available service units, leaving a reserve of roughly 247,000 whose first call is an additional ensemble draw; the variable-resolution sweep is deprioritized and runs only on whatever allocation remains, and the optional second test-ensemble construction is a scope decision against the same reserve. Each combination of design, draw, and seed is an independent job, and every function evaluation simulates the trimmed model over the design's 100-realization ensemble. Runtime archive snapshots are logged at a fixed NFE interval for the convergence diagnostics of Section 5.3, and the epsilon-dominance precisions are the calibrated per-objective values of Table 2, shared by every design (Supporting Information Section S6). The full campaign geometry and the service-unit accounting are reported in Supporting Information Section S8.

## 8. Anticipated Threats to Validity

Five threats structure how the results will be read, and we state them before any result exists.

The first threat is the possibility that designed selection provides no benefit. Eker and Kwakkel (2018) found that diversity-based scenario selection did not outperform randomly selected scenarios on the lake problem. The contrast between `fixed_probabilistic` and `hazard_filling` is that benchmark with replicated ensemble draws and on a real system: our selection is in hazard space rather than in outcome space on a stylized benchmark, and the comparison rests on independent ensemble draws with the draw as the unit of analysis rather than on solution counting. If hazard filling does not outperform random sampling under this design, the null is reported as such, against the observed draw- and seed-level variability. The reframing of this study around a single controlled contrast, rather than a larger set of designs, is deliberate: it makes that null, if it occurs, interpretable.

The second threat is the search-measure mismatch penalty. Giuliani and Castelletti (2016) show that policies designed under one across-scenario measure and evaluated under another are systematically dominated by policies designed under the evaluation measure. Hazard filling searches under a distribution deliberately shifted toward severe conditions and is scored under E_test's composition, so the mismatch penalty is the expected direction of the effect under the null hypothesis, and any hazard-filling advantage must overcome it. We state this as the study's null hypothesis rather than leaving it to be identified in review.

The third threat is selection bias, which this study measures rather than corrects. Bartholomew and Kwakkel (2020) articulate the objection that applies to any designed scenario selection, writing that it "intrinsically biases subsequent results towards solutions that do well in this region," with no a priori guarantee against vulnerability elsewhere. We do not dispute this. The distribution shift is deliberate, stated, and coverage-motivated; the held-out re-evaluation corrects evaluation bias only; and the residual selection effect, including any induced vulnerability in under-represented regions, is precisely the quantity the experiment measures on the common test ensemble.

The fourth threat concerns conditioning and scope. All rankings are conditional on the single E_test construction (Section 6.1); robustness values are coverage-weighted counts rather than expectations; the forcing space perturbs the monthly mean profile, with relative variability preserved, but not interannual persistence, so every claim is scoped to hold under historical-level interannual persistence; and NYC and NJ demands are held at the constant Decree maxima, so no demand-growth axis enters either search or test. Each claim is therefore conditional on this basin, this rule structure, this objective set, and this demand representation. The two ensemble designs also differ in the variance of their fitness estimates, since a frozen i.i.d. ensemble and a frozen coverage-designed ensemble present different sampling structures to the optimizer; this is matched in budget but not in kind, and is disclosed rather than removed. The design most exposed to a favorable-instrument objection under the mismatch-penalty logic would be one whose search distribution resembled E_test, and neither matched design is constructed that way: both search on the unperturbed generator while E_test spans a forced envelope, so E_test does not structurally favor either design over the other.

The fifth threat concerns mechanism attribution and the boundary of practice guidance. The hazard-filling construction is a bundle: relative to i.i.d. sampling it simultaneously enriches the severe tail of every selection axis, separates members so that no two occupy the same stratum, and depletes typical conditions. A positive RQ1 result therefore establishes that designed hazard-space selection outperforms random sampling from the same population; it does not by itself attribute the effect to coverage rather than to tail enrichment alone, and prior stress-oriented selection results suggest enrichment may carry much of any effect (Hilbers et al., 2019; Cohen et al., 2021). The registered rank-space variant (Section 4.4), which reproduces the pool's marginal frequencies while retaining the space-filling construction, is the mechanism probe, and it sits outside the campaign. For the same reason the practice guidance is bounded: both matched designs search on the stationary generator, so the comparison cannot exclude that a search ensemble spanning the deeply uncertain forcing space itself — the robustness-in-search default of Trindade et al. (2017) and Bartholomew and Kwakkel (2020) — would outperform both. The question answered is whether hazard-informed selection beats probability-faithful sampling from a common population, not which of all possible search ensembles is best.
